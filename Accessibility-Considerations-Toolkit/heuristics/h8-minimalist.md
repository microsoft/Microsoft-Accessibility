# 8. Aesthetic and Minimalist Design

**Definition**: Interfaces should not contain information that is irrelevant or rarely needed. Every
extra unit of information in an interface competes with the relevant units of information and
diminishes their relative visibility.

**Accessibility Lens**: Visual clutter may also create auditory or navigational clutter when exposed
through AT. Redundant descriptions ('image of image of photo'), overly verbose text alternatives,
and decorative elements surfaced to the user all create noise. Every element the user encounters is
something they must process or skip past. Decorative content should not be surfaced to assistive
technology users. Aim for the right balance: names and descriptions should be informative without
being verbose. The flip side of minimalism is perceivability: a critical element that cannot be
perceived — either absent from the user's experience entirely, or effectively invisible due to poor
naming, missing identification, or burial in noise — is worse than clutter. Competing stimuli that
divert attention (intrusive announcements, auto-playing media, modal popups interrupting a task)
actively prevent the user from focusing on what matters. Minimalism means removing the irrelevant
and ensuring the relevant is prominent. Anything considered unimportant for someone to notice or
understand should raise the question of whether to include it at all.
