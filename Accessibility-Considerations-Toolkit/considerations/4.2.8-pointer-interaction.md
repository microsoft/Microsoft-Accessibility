# FPS 4.2.8 — Usage with limited reach

Where ICT products are freestanding or installed, the operational elements shall be within reach of
all users.

**Scope for screen-based evaluation**: The EN 301 549 statement addresses physical reach to hardware
controls (kiosks, ATMs, mounted screens, physical buttons), which is out of scope for software
interface evaluation. Evaluate the adjacent _software_ requirements for **pointing-device operation
with limited dexterity, strength, or range of motion** — touchscreen, trackball, adapted mouse, head
pointer, eye-gaze, mouth/head stick, or switch-driven pointer — including fixed-mounted devices.
Test precise, sustained, and multi-step pointer demands without assuming clear vision or the
availability of keyboard or voice fallback. This software lens is also relevant to 4.2.7's
manipulation/strength concerns; it is not the normative physical reach requirement and cannot
establish compliance with 4.2.8.

This consideration complements keyboard-only evaluation (4.2.7, alias: keyboard-interaction) and
voice-access evaluation (4.2.7, alias: voice-control), which exclude manual pointer fallback.
Evaluate target size and spacing, single-pointer operability, alternatives to dragging and complex
gestures, accidental-activation recovery, transient hover/focus content, and independence from
device motion or a fixed orientation.

## Evaluation Considerations

Evaluate **pointing-device** paths where target acquisition is slower or imprecise, holding while
moving is difficult or unavailable, or accidental activation occurs. These are possible interaction
constraints, not a simulated person's universal attributes. Do not assume unrestricted vision,
visual-layout comprehension, or absence of other access requirements.

Consider EACH of these interaction contexts:

1. Low-precision trackball or adapted-mouse input, including possible tremor or spasticity:
   small-target overshoot, double activation, or drift before release
2. Head-pointer or eye-gaze dwell activation configured without path-based swipes, multi-finger
   gestures, or press-and-hold dragging; dwelling over an object may activate it
3. A fixed-mounted device (e.g. on a power wheelchair) that cannot be tilted, shaken, or rotated in
   the evaluated setup

For each screenshot, consider:

- Are TARGETS large enough and far enough apart to acquire without hitting a neighbour? Are there
  tightly packed icon buttons, small close (×) controls, or dense menus?
- Does any function require a PATH-BASED or MULTI-POINT GESTURE (swipe, pinch-zoom, flick,
  draw-a-shape) with no single-tap/click alternative?
- Does any function require DRAGGING (drag-and-drop, slider thumb, drag-to-reorder, drag-to-dismiss)
  with no click/tap alternative?
- What happens on ACCIDENTAL ACTIVATION? Does the action fire on the DOWN-event (so the user cannot
  abort by moving off before release), or on the up-event with an abort/undo path?
- Does any function require DEVICE MOTION (shake, tilt, gesture-to-camera) without an equivalent
  on-screen control, and can motion actuation be turned off?
- Is the content LOCKED to a single ORIENTATION, forcing a user with a fixed-mounted device to
  operate it sideways?
- Does important content appear only on HOVER or FOCUS, and can the user move onto it, keep it
  visible, and dismiss it without disrupting their pointer position?
- Are there TIME-LIMITED interactions (auto-closing menus, disappearing tooltips, timeouts) that
  disadvantage a slow, deliberate pointer user?

Ground findings in target geometry and tested pointer behavior. Focus on single-pointer operability,
gesture and drag alternatives, recoverability, motion, and orientation while retaining the explicit
software/physical-reach boundary. Pointer use does not establish a person's sensory, cognitive, or
motor abilities.

## Assistive Technologies

### Touchscreen operated with limited dexterity

**Category**: touch **Platforms**: iOS, Android, Windows **Device context**: Mobile, Desktop

Test direct touchscreen single taps with reduced precision, possible overshoot, double-taps, and
release drift that may occur with tremor, spasticity, or weakness. Multi-finger gestures (pinch,
two-finger rotate), path-based swipes, press-and-hold, and dragging may be difficult or unavailable.
Direct touch provides no hover preview before a tap.

### Trackball or adapted / oversized mouse

**Category**: pointer **Platforms**: Windows, macOS, Linux **Device context**: Desktop

Trackballs, joystick mice, oversized ergonomic mice, and button-separated devices can reduce
strength and fine motor demands. Separating movement and clicking can limit accidental drag.
Evaluate holding while repositioning, overshoot of small or closely spaced targets, and possible
declining click accuracy with fatigue; these constraints vary by device and configuration.

### Head-tracking pointer (head pointer)

**Category**: pointer **Platforms**: Windows, macOS, iOS, Android **Device context**: Desktop,
Mobile

A camera or reflective-dot tracker translates head motion into pointer position. Activation is
typically by dwell (resting on a target for a set time) or a separate switch. In the evaluated
configuration, path-based gestures and press-and-hold dragging may be unavailable; resting on a
control may activate it. Test small-target acquisition and the head-movement effort it requires.

### Eye-gaze pointer

**Category**: pointer **Platforms**: Windows, iOS, Android **Device context**: Desktop, Mobile

An eye-tracking camera maps gaze to pointer coordinates; activation uses dwell or a separate switch.
The tracked point can jitter around the gaze target, making small or closely spaced controls harder
to acquire. Test configurations without dragging or multi-point gestures and transient content that
appears or disappears as gaze moves; do not generalize those configuration boundaries to every gaze
system.

### Mouth stick / head stick

**Category**: touch **Platforms**: iOS, Android, Windows **Device context**: Mobile, Desktop

A mouth-held or head-mounted stick operates touchscreen controls or physical keys through
single-point contacts. Multi-finger gestures are unavailable through that contact method; sustained
dragging can be fatiguing or imprecise. Test the comfortably reachable area and distant control
clusters without treating screen geometry as proof of normative hardware reach compliance.

### Switch-driven pointer / scanning

**Category**: switch **Platforms**: Windows, macOS, iOS, Android **Device context**: Desktop, Mobile

One or two switches drive a scanning pointer or directional cursor, stepping a highlight or moving
across regions and targets before switch selection. Evaluate single-point, step-based configurations
without path-based gestures, multi-point gestures, or press-and-hold dragging. Multiple activations
per selection can make dense layouts and long sequences slow or tiring.

### Wheelchair-mounted device (fixed orientation)

**Category**: mounting **Platforms**: iOS, Android, Windows **Device context**: Mobile, Desktop

The user's device is physically mounted — for example on the arm of a power wheelchair — so it
cannot be picked up, tilted, shaken, or rotated. The mounting fixes the display in one orientation
(often landscape), and motion-based input (shake-to-undo, tilt-to-scroll, gesture-to-camera) is
unavailable because the device cannot be moved. Content that locks to the opposite orientation, or
that depends on device motion, is unusable in this configuration.

## Exploration Strategies

### Single-pointer activation without complex gestures

Test single-pointer paths using tap, click, double-tap, long-press, click-and-hold, or dwell as
available in the configuration. These actions are not equally usable in every context. Path-based
gestures (swipe, flick, draw-a-shape) and multi-point gestures (two-finger pinch, two-finger rotate,
split-tap) may be unavailable for head-pointer, eye-gaze, switch, or single-stick input;
gesture-only functions can then be blocked entirely.

**AT Mechanisms**: Single tap / click, Double tap / click, Long press, Dwell activation **Device
context**: All

**UI Requirements**: All functionality that uses a multipoint or path-based gesture must also be
operable with a single pointer without a path (e.g. a pinch-zoom map also offers +/− buttons; a
swipe carousel also offers prev/next buttons). Custom-coded swipe, flick, and pinch handlers must
not be the only way to reach a function unless the gesture is genuinely essential (e.g. a freehand
signature).

### Avoiding and replacing dragging

Dragging coordinates press, hold, movement, and release. Evaluate whether that sequence is
unavailable or difficult with low-precision, head-pointer, eye-gaze, switch, or stick input. Test
non-drag paths: tap-to-select then tap-the-destination, up/down reorder buttons, click-on-track
sliders, or numeric input beside a slider or color wheel.

**AT Mechanisms**: Tap-select then tap-target, Click on slider track, Reorder buttons, Numeric entry
**Device context**: All

**UI Requirements**: All functionality that uses a dragging movement must be achievable by a single
pointer without dragging — slider tracks accept a click/tap to set the value, drag-to-reorder lists
offer move buttons, drag-and-drop offers a select-then-place or menu alternative, and
drag-to-dismiss offers a close button. Keyboard equivalence alone does not satisfy this need; a
pointer-clickable alternative must exist.

### Acquiring small and closely-spaced targets

Evaluate acquisition effort and neighbouring-target risk for tiny close × controls, icon-only
toolbar buttons, compact dropdown rows, and dense clusters. Low-precision or gaze input may
overshoot; switch scanning adds steps for extra targets. Generous size and spacing can reduce
retries and unintended activation.

**AT Mechanisms**: Pointer acquisition, Dwell activation, Scanning highlight **Device context**: All

**UI Requirements**: Pointer targets are at least 24×24 CSS pixels, or undersized targets have
enough spacing that a 24px-diameter circle centred on each does not overlap a neighbour. Critical or
frequently used controls should be more generous still. Avoid placing tiny destructive controls
(delete, close, remove) immediately adjacent to other targets.

### Recovering from accidental activation

Test accidental activation with imprecise pointing, dwell, and switches. _Down_-event completion
removes the opportunity to move off before release; _up_-event activation can provide that
cancellation path where supported. Evaluate explicit abort or undo for multi-step actions rather
than assuming every input configuration supports move-off cancellation.

**AT Mechanisms**: Up-event release, Move-off-to-cancel, Undo / abort control **Device context**:
All

**UI Requirements**: Functions operated by a single pointer do not complete on the down-event
(except where down-event timing is genuinely essential, e.g. an on-screen piano key); they complete
on the up-event, with a way to abort before completion or undo afterward. Destructive actions are
reversible or confirmed. Drag operations can be aborted by releasing outside a drop target.

### Operating without device motion

Test shake-to-undo, tilt-to-scroll, and camera gestures where device or user motion is unavailable,
including fixed-mounted setups. Tremor may cause unintended motion actuation. Check conventional
on-screen equivalents and the ability to disable motion input.

**AT Mechanisms**: On-screen control equivalents, Motion-input disable setting **Device context**:
Mobile

**UI Requirements**: Any function operable by device or user motion is also operable through
standard UI components, and motion response can be disabled to prevent accidental actuation
(supporting the OS-level motion-disable setting satisfies this). Motion-only functions (with no
on-screen equivalent) are a barrier unless the motion is genuinely essential to the activity.

### Working with a fixed device orientation

Test a fixed-mounted device, often landscape on a wheelchair arm, without rotating it. Content
locked to the opposite orientation or a "please rotate your device" gate can block the setup.
Evaluate adaptation to the device's actual orientation rather than assume every mount has identical
movement limits.

**AT Mechanisms**: Responsive layout, Device orientation setting **Device context**: Mobile

**UI Requirements**: Content does not restrict its view and operation to a single display
orientation (portrait or landscape) unless a specific orientation is essential. The layout works in
both orientations, and the interface never blocks use until the device is rotated.

### Managing transient hover or focus content

Test tooltips, sub-menus, and popovers with large or jittering head/gaze pointers. Pointer movement
may obscure, retrigger, or dismiss content, and crossing a trigger may summon unwanted overlays.
Evaluate stable, reachable, dismissible content without precise pointer choreography.

**AT Mechanisms**: Pointer hover, Dwell, Dismiss control (Escape / close) **Device context**:
Desktop, Mobile

**UI Requirements**: Content shown on hover or focus is **dismissible** (can be removed without
moving the pointer, e.g. via Escape), **hoverable** (the pointer can move onto the content without
it disappearing), and **persistent** (it stays until the trigger is removed, the user dismisses it,
or it becomes invalid). Essential information must not be hover-only.

### Visual scanning and layout comprehension _(from shared)_

When visual scanning is available and preferred, page regions such as the header, navigation, main
content, sidebar, and footer can help establish layout. Visible control affordances and hierarchy
through headings, spacing, colour, and size may help locate actions. Do not assume that visual
scanning is available or that these cues work alone.

**AT Mechanisms**: Visual perception **Device context**: All

**UI Requirements**: Clear visual hierarchy through headings, spacing, and grouping. Interactive
elements must look interactive (visible borders, underlines, or other affordances). Primary actions
should be visually prominent.

### Mouse / pointer interaction _(from shared)_

Mouse, trackpad, or touch-pointer interaction may involve clicking, selecting text, dragging,
hovering for tooltips, or opening context menus. Evaluate the actions supported by the chosen input
method; hover and sustained dragging are not available in every pointer context.

**AT Mechanisms**: Mouse, Trackpad, Touchscreen **Device context**: Desktop

**UI Requirements**: Interactive elements are easy to activate with the intended pointer action and
are far enough apart that users do not accidentally activate adjacent controls. Hover states should
indicate interactivity. Drag-and-drop should have clear affordances.

### Reading and scrolling _(from shared)_

Visual reading may proceed sequentially or through heading scans, with scrolling to reach content
beyond the viewport. Evaluate logical flow appropriate to the writing system, such as top-to-bottom
and left-to-right in LTR content. Do not assume one reading direction or scrolling method across
languages and input configurations.

**AT Mechanisms**: Visual reading, Mouse wheel / scroll gesture **Device context**: All

**UI Requirements**: Text must be readable: sufficient contrast, reasonable font size, adequate line
spacing. Content should not require horizontal scrolling at standard viewport widths. Long pages
should provide navigation aids (table of contents, back-to-top).

### Form interaction _(from shared)_

Form tasks may involve reaching fields, typing, selecting options, checking boxes, and submitting
through different input methods. Evaluate clear labels, placeholder text that does not replace
labels, and actionable error feedback without assuming pointer access.

**AT Mechanisms**: Keyboard (typing), Mouse (clicking fields), Visual reading (labels) **Device
context**: All

**UI Requirements**: Every form field must have a visible label. Required fields must be clearly
indicated. Error messages must be specific, adjacent to the field, and visible without scrolling.
Successful submission must be clearly confirmed.

### Navigation and wayfinding _(from shared)_

Menus, breadcrumbs, links, and browser back/forward can support navigation between pages and views.
Evaluate whether the current location, available destinations, and return paths are understandable
through the access strategies in scope.

**AT Mechanisms**: Visual scanning, Mouse clicking, Browser controls **Device context**: All

**UI Requirements**: Consistent navigation across pages. Current location clearly indicated (active
nav item, breadcrumbs). Important actions reachable from any page. Browser back button should work
predictably.

### Touch / pointer interaction _(from shared)_

Touch interaction may include taps, swipes, pinch-zoom, or long presses, depending on the device and
access method. Direct touch has no hover preview; evaluate discoverable controls, target size, and
alternatives to complex gestures. Do not require every touch interaction pattern to be available to
the same person.

**AT Mechanisms**: Touchscreen tap, Swipe gestures, Pinch-zoom **Device context**: Mobile

**UI Requirements**: Touch targets are easy to activate without accidentally activating adjacent
controls — small or tightly packed targets force users to retry or hit the wrong control. No
reliance on hover for essential information. Custom gestures must have single-pointer alternatives.
Drag-and-drop must have tap-based alternatives. No accidental activation from scroll gestures.

## Interaction Contexts

### Low-precision pointer input (tremor / spasticity contexts)

Evaluate trackball, adapted-mouse, or direct-touch input with possible overshoot, release drift, and
double activation. Essential tremor, cerebral palsy, multiple sclerosis, or Parkinson's may be
relevant contexts but do not determine a person's interaction pattern. Check generous target size
and spacing, up-event move-off cancellation, and non-drag alternatives.

**Primary AT**: Trackball or adapted / oversized mouse **Device context**: Desktop, Mobile
**Preferred navigation**: Acquiring small and closely-spaced targets, Recovering from accidental
activation, Avoiding and replacing dragging

**Expectations**:

- Targets large and well-spaced enough to hit without catching a neighbour
- Actions fire on release, with a way to abort by moving off before releasing
- No function that depends on dragging, pinching, or path-based swipes
- Destructive actions are confirmed or reversible
- Tolerance for accidental double-activation

### Head-pointer / eye-gaze dwell activation

Evaluate head-motion or gaze positioning with dwell activation and no manual pointing fallback. In
configurations without path-based or multi-point gestures or press-and-hold dragging, test
single-dwell alternatives. Positional activation can fire on resting targets; check well-separated
controls and overlays that remain stable as the pointer crosses the screen.

**Primary AT**: Head-tracking pointer or Eye-gaze pointer **Device context**: Desktop, Mobile
**Preferred navigation**: Single-pointer activation without complex gestures, Acquiring small and
closely-spaced targets, Managing transient hover or focus content

**Expectations**:

- Every function reachable by single dwell activation — no gestures, no dragging
- Targets large and isolated enough that dwell lands on the intended control
- Hover/focus content is persistent, hoverable, and dismissible
- No reliance on device motion where movement is unavailable in the head-mounted or gaze setup
- Forgiving activation that tolerates jitter around the intended target

### Fixed wheelchair-mounted device operation

Evaluate a power-wheelchair-mounted device operated by stick, switch, or limited-range touch. In a
fixed setup the device cannot be picked up, tilted, shaken, or rotated. Screen regions may be
outside comfortable reach. Check orientation-agnostic layouts, motion equivalents, and control
placement, while reserving physical reach compliance for hardware assessment.

**Primary AT**: Wheelchair-mounted device (fixed orientation), Switch-driven pointer / scanning
**Device context**: Mobile, Desktop **Preferred navigation**: Working with a fixed device
orientation, Operating without device motion, Single-pointer activation without complex gestures

**Expectations**:

- Content works in the device's fixed orientation — never blocked behind "rotate your device"
- All motion-triggered functions have on-screen control equivalents and motion input can be disabled
- Important controls are not clustered in far screen corners out of reach
- Single-pointer / switch operable throughout
- No time pressure that penalises slow, deliberate selection

### New / infrequent user _(from shared)_

First-time or infrequent interaction may require rediscovering controls and workflows. Labels,
accessible affordances, and progressive disclosure can support learning without requiring prior
knowledge of shortcuts or hidden features.

**Primary AT**: None **Device context**: All **Preferred navigation**: Visual scanning and layout
comprehension, Navigation and wayfinding, Form interaction

**Expectations**:

- Interface is learnable without documentation
- Primary actions are visually obvious
- Error messages guide toward resolution
- Navigation structure is understandable

### Expert / frequent user _(from shared)_

Frequent interaction may build familiarity with layouts, shortcuts, and workflows. Consistency and
efficient paths can support established strategies; changes may require relearning. Expertise in a
task does not establish expertise with every assistive technology.

**Primary AT**: None **Device context**: All **Preferred navigation**: Mouse / pointer interaction,
Navigation and wayfinding

**Expectations**:

- Keyboard shortcuts for common tasks
- Consistent layout across sessions and pages
- Efficient paths to frequently used features
- Changes to familiar workflows are clearly communicated

## Learning Considerations

- **Pointer acquisition has a physical cost**: Acquisition may involve careful head movement, a
  steadied trackball roll, a dwell wait, or several switch steps. Small targets, dense clusters, and
  long sequences can accumulate fatigue and error. Evaluate the number and precision of required
  actions alongside basic operability.

- **Dwell and positional activation can cause false triggers**: In dwell configurations, resting on
  controls or overlays appearing under the pointer may trigger activation. Evaluate deliberate
  spacing, generous targets, and persistent rather than auto-appearing content to reduce this risk.

- **Abort and undo are safety nets, not extras**: Accidental activation may recur with imprecise
  input. Evaluate up-event move-off cancellation where supported, destructive-action confirmation,
  and reliable undo. Down-event completion removes the pre-release cancellation path, not
  necessarily every possible recovery option.

- **Dragging and gestures can be hard dead-ends**: A slider, reorder list, drag-and-drop
  interaction, or pinch-zoom map may be completely blocked when its required motion is unavailable.
  Test single-pointer alternatives rather than comparing assumed capabilities of different people.

- **Mounted and fixed setups can remove input channels**: In a fixed wheelchair-mounted setup, tilt,
  shake, or rotation may be unavailable. Motion-only functions or incompatible orientation locks can
  completely block operation; assess the setup rather than generalize to all mounted devices.

- **First visit** _(from shared)_: Familiar web, desktop, and mobile conventions may support
  learning, but recognition of hamburger menus, search icons, tabs, or modals is not guaranteed.
  Explain unfamiliar patterns and expose their purpose through accessible labels and feedback.

- **Mental models** _(from shared)_: Early interactions may establish expectations. Inconsistent
  behavior, such as a button that sometimes navigates and sometimes submits, can undermine
  confidence and require additional checking.

- **Error recovery** _(from shared)_: Support recovery from mistakes through undo, appropriate
  confirmation dialogs, and clear error messages. Make the consequences of irreversible actions
  clear rather than assuming every mistake can be avoided.

- **Cognitive load** _(from shared)_: Clutter, excessive options, and unclear hierarchy can increase
  attention and memory demands. Evaluate those demands in the task context without inferring a
  person's cognitive capabilities.

## Heuristic Lens Overrides

### Heuristic 1 — Visibility of System Status

Evaluate immediate feedback after tap, dwell, switch scan, or careful pointer movement, particularly
where activation is slower or uncertain. Pressed/selected states and loading indicators near the
control can reduce retries and double activation. Distant feedback may fall outside the region
attended through gaze or magnification; assess the relevant configuration without assuming uniform
perception.

### Heuristic 2 — Match Between System and Real World

Evaluate familiar single-pointer controls: slider track clicks, carousel prev/next buttons, and list
move controls. Buttons and links should work through simple tap/click paths. Hidden swipe-to-reveal
or pinch-to-act requirements may conflict with visible affordances; do not assume prior knowledge of
hidden gestures.

### Heuristic 3 — User Control and Freedom

Evaluate recovery when accidental activation occurs. Up-event completion can permit move-off
cancellation; multi-step and destructive actions need explicit abort or undo. Modals and overlays
need a single generously sized dismiss control, dragging must abort outside a drop zone, and motion
responses must be disableable. Can recovery avoid precise follow-up actions? Retain genuinely
essential down-event exceptions described above.

### Heuristic 4 — Consistency and Standards

Evaluate consistent single-pointer alternatives and target sizing. Sliders that alternate track
clicks and drag-only operation, or lists that alternate move buttons and drag-and-drop-only paths,
may require risky trial or block particular components. Consistent up-event activation, dismiss
controls, and sizing can support learned operation without assuming a uniform motor routine.

### Heuristic 5 — Error Prevention

Evaluate targets at least 24×24 CSS pixels or spacing with non-overlapping 24px circles to reduce
neighbouring-target risk; geometry does not guarantee error-free activation. Separate small
destructive controls, offer single-pointer alternatives to drags and gestures, and confirm
irreversible actions. Check tiny close (×) buttons, dense toolbars, and stacked menu rows.

### Heuristic 6 — Recognition Rather than Recall

Evaluate persistent buttons, labels, and on-screen equivalents for gestures or motion. Swipe-,
long-press-, or shake-only paths may require both recall and an unavailable movement. Visible
alternatives should provide a recognizable operable path without requiring that motor sequence.

### Heuristic 7 — Flexibility and Efficiency of Use

Provide lower-precision pointer paths, such as slider track clicks, numeric entry, and
tap-to-select-then-place. Assess fewer, larger, forgiving targets and shorter sequences against
varied effort budgets. Keyboard shortcuts alone do not address a pointer-only test path, though they
may be useful in other contexts.

### Heuristic 8 — Aesthetic and Minimalist Design

Evaluate density as an activation and scanning burden: extra controls add neighbouring targets and
switch-scan steps. Generous spacing, fewer competing targets, and room around primary/destructive
controls can reduce mis-activation. Compact layouts should not depend on high pointing precision.

### Heuristic 9 — Help Users Recognize, Diagnose, and Recover from Errors

When an accidental activation or a mistaken entry occurs, recovery controls must be easy to operate
with an imprecise pointer — large, well-spaced, and clearly labelled (an undo button the user can
actually hit, a generous "Cancel"). Error messages and their associated fix actions should be
reachable without precise dragging or gesture. The recovery path must tolerate the input imprecision
being evaluated rather than attribute errors to a person's capability.

### Heuristic 10 — Help and Documentation

If the product offers any gesture-, drag-, or motion-based interaction, documentation and in-product
hints should make the single-pointer alternative discoverable, so the user knows they are not
required to perform the motion. Help content and any instructional overlays must themselves be
operable with a single pointer and dismissible with a generously sized control. Instructions that
only describe the gesture path (e.g. "swipe to delete") without mentioning the button alternative
leave the user assuming a function is out of reach.
