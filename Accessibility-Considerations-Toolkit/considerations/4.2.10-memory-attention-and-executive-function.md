# FPS 4.2.10 — Usage with limited cognition, language or learning (memory, attention, and executive function)

The ICT provides features and/or presentation that makes it simpler and easier to understand,
operate, and use.

## Evaluation Considerations

Evaluate possible barriers to holding information, sustaining and recovering attention, planning and
sequencing multi-step tasks, and learning or recalling interface patterns. Requirements vary and may
overlap with sensory, motor, language, and numeracy requirements. Do not simulate a person or assume
unrelated capabilities are unaffected.

Test reduced working-memory demand, including an illustrative context of one to three pieces of
information at once, not a universal capability limit. Cross-screen code transcription, recalling a
choice from two steps earlier, or remembering a password may present barriers. Notifications, moving
banners, or interruptions can disrupt task context. New patterns may require substantial or repeated
learning, and may remain inaccessible in some contexts; evaluate familiar conventions and
re-orientation support rather than predicting learning capacity.

For each screen or flow, consider:

- **ORIENT**: Can the user tell where they are, what this page is for, and what they were doing —
  especially after a distraction? Are there persistent headings, breadcrumbs, and step indicators,
  or a "how did I get here" affordance?
- **HOLD**: Does completing the task require remembering information across steps or screens (a
  code, a prior choice, an instruction that vanished)? Is everything needed to act present at the
  point of use?
- **FOCUS**: Are there interruptions, autoplaying media, moving or shifting content, or visual
  clutter that pull attention away? Can distractions be paused, dismissed, or turned off?
- **SEQUENCE**: Are multi-step processes broken into clear, minimal steps with visible progress? Are
  optional steps separated from required ones rather than forced into the path?
- **CONFIRM**: After each action, is there immediate, unambiguous feedback that it worked (or
  failed)? Or could the user be left unsure whether anything happened?
- **RECOVER**: Can the user go back, undo, and correct mistakes without losing entered work? Does
  the Back control behave predictably? Are timeouts avoided, or at least warned about generously at
  the start?
- **LEARN**: Does the interface rely on familiar, conventional patterns the user already knows, or
  does it demand learning and remembering new ones? Are controls, terms, and layouts consistent
  across the experience?

Possible access barriers include: authentication that depends on remembering or transcribing
passwords and codes; multi-step forms that do not carry information forward; labels and instructions
that disappear once a field has focus; sessions that time out while the user looks something up;
content that moves or reflows unexpectedly under the pointer; distracting interruptions that cannot
be turned off; actions that complete (or fail) with no feedback; an unpredictable Back button; and
dense, cluttered layouts with too many choices.

IMPORTANT — Audience-appropriate language and terminology:

Assess clarity relative to the interface's actual user base and domain, not against a universal
"simplest possible words" standard. Specialized terminology that is fundamental to the experience
and familiar to its intended users — for example, resource and namespace names in a Kubernetes
administration console, or instrument and parameter names in a scientific analysis tool — is NOT a
clarity failure. Do not flag domain-correct vocabulary as "unclear words" for an audience that knows
and needs those terms.

For this consideration the memory-related concern is different from vocabulary difficulty: it is
whether labels, instructions, and steps stay **stable, consistent, and available at the point of
use** so the user does not have to remember them. Flag the same concept being named two different
ways on two screens; instructions or labels that vanish on focus; gratuitous jargon or coined terms
that are not domain-required and have no one-click explanation; and terminology that shifts between
steps. Necessary domain terms should still be supported — consistent usage, persistent visible
labels, and on-demand definitions — but their mere presence is not a defect.

## Assistive Technologies

Evaluate a varied mix of mainstream features, browser extensions, and tools that offload memory or
reduce distraction, rather than assume a single AT or shared tool preference. Do not disable these
supports; also test usability without specialized tools configured.

### Reminder and calendar tools

**Category**: reminder-tool **Platforms**: Windows, macOS, iOS, Android, Web **Device context**: All

Calendar apps, task managers, and notification/reminder systems offload dates, deadlines, and steps.
Evaluate user-requested "add to calendar", downloadable `.ics` events, or standard
reminder/notification hooks. Forced manual copying of appointment times can introduce transcription
errors.

### Password managers

**Category**: credential-manager **Platforms**: Windows, macOS, iOS, Android, browser extensions
**Device context**: All

Password managers store and auto-fill credentials without recall, invention, or transcription. This
may be an essential independent-login path where memory demands are barriers. Permit paste and
autofill; blocking paste, fragmenting a code across fields, or demanding memorized strings,
hand-copied one-time codes, or multi-step recall can defeat that path.

### Reading, de-clutter, and focus extensions

**Category**: simplification **Platforms**: Chrome, Edge, Firefox, Safari (desktop and mobile)
**Device context**: Desktop, Mobile

Reader-mode and distraction-removing extensions strip advertisements, moving content, and secondary
clutter; focus tools mute notifications. These may support attention or reduce competing choices.
Evaluate reader mode, regenerated clutter, and custom widgets that extensions cannot simplify; tool
preferences and effectiveness vary.

### Form autofill and autocomplete

**Category**: form-autofill **Platforms**: Browsers, OS-level autofill (Windows, macOS, iOS,
Android) **Device context**: All

Browser and platform autofill re-enter routine name, address, phone, and email information, reducing
recall and retyping demands. Test standard HTML autocomplete semantics and conventional field names.
Custom or mislabeled fields may prevent recognition and force manual recall or re-entry.

### Text-to-speech / read-aloud

**Category**: read-aloud **Platforms**: Windows, macOS, iOS, Android, browser extensions **Device
context**: All

Read-aloud tools speak content, sometimes with synchronized word highlighting, and may reduce
repeated reading or working-memory demand. Evaluate comprehension and re-orientation support without
treating read-aloud use as evidence about vision; purposes may overlap. Test real selectable text in
logical reading order, not images of text or scattered fragments.

### Personalization and simplification semantics

**Category**: personalization **Platforms**: Browser, extensions, OS preferences **Device context**:
All

Personalization tools and simplification toolbars can apply saved preferences for a simplified
version, fewer features, extra spacing, familiar layout, or symbols through exposed semantics. Users
or chosen supporters, including caregivers, may configure these once for reuse. Evaluate adaptation
hooks and a simpler mode preserving the critical path rather than a single hard-coded dense
presentation.

### Built-in platform memory aids

**Category**: platform-feature **Platforms**: All **Device context**: All

Evaluate standard platform features as memory aids: Back and history, undo, autosave and drafts,
recently-used lists, and system notifications. Overridden conventions, Back that loses data, missing
undo or autosave, and processes that cannot resume after interruption may remove these supports.

## Exploration Strategies

### Orientation and re-orientation

Test orientation and return after interruptions using page titles, purpose headings, breadcrumbs,
step indicators, and "how did I get here" or summary affordances. Missing signposts may prevent
resumption where task context is hard to retain; interruptions do not affect every person
identically.

**AT Mechanisms**: Visual reading, Read-aloud, Browser history / Back **Device context**: All

**UI Requirements**: Every screen has a clear, descriptive heading that states its purpose.
Multi-step processes show completed, current, and remaining steps. Location is indicated (active nav
item, breadcrumbs). Context needed to resume a task is preserved and visible, not held only in the
user's memory.

### Chunked task progression

Evaluate small-step progression with everything needed at each step, visible progress, and a short
critical path. Later steps assuming remembered earlier values, or many bundled steps on one screen,
may overload working memory or disrupt sequencing. Do not declare a fixed capacity to hold an entire
task.

**AT Mechanisms**: Visual reading, Form autofill, Reminder tools **Device context**: All

**UI Requirements**: Break long processes into clear, minimal, labeled steps. Each step carries
forward the information it needs — never rely on the user remembering a value from a previous step.
Show progress. Separate optional steps from the required critical path.

### Recognition over recall navigation

Evaluate recognition paths using conventional search, home, and menu placement and persistent labels
instead of required recall of paths, shortcuts, or hidden gestures. Custom navigation, hover-only
features, and memorized routes may add discovery or learning barriers. Familiarity with conventions
varies.

**AT Mechanisms**: Visual scanning, Mouse / pointer, Touch **Device context**: All

**UI Requirements**: Use conventional, consistently placed navigation and controls. Keep important
actions visibly labeled rather than hidden behind discovery. Do not require memorized shortcuts or
gestures to reach essential functions; offer a visible alternative.

### Distraction management

Test pausing carousels and autoplay, blocking or muting notifications, and using reader or focus
modes. Competing stimuli may displace task context and prevent completion in some circumstances.
Evaluate control over distractions without assuming a common attention pattern.

**AT Mechanisms**: Reading / focus extensions, OS focus modes **Device context**: All

**UI Requirements**: Provide easy control over interruptions, moving content, and notifications
(pause, dismiss, or turn off), except where genuinely an emergency. Avoid content that animates,
autoplays, or shifts without the user initiating it. Keep non-essential content from competing with
the task.

### Offloading to external aids

Test offloading dates and steps to reminders/calendars, routine data to autofill/password managers,
and choices or context to notes. Check add-to-calendar and autocomplete hooks, paste, and saved-data
persistence. Required recall or regenerated/invalidated data can defeat these supports.

**AT Mechanisms**: Reminder tools, Password managers, Form autofill **Device context**: All

**UI Requirements**: Support standard mechanisms for offloading — calendar/reminder hooks, HTML
autocomplete, paste into all fields. Preserve and auto-save the user's entered data. Do not require
recall of anything the system already knows or could carry forward.

### Feedback-confirmed action

Evaluate explicit acknowledgement after actions: a confirmation message, changed button state, or
completed-step checkmark. When action completion is difficult to recall, missing feedback may cause
uncertainty, duplicate submission, or abandonment.

**AT Mechanisms**: Visual reading, Read-aloud, Audio cues **Device context**: All

**UI Requirements**: Every user action produces immediate, clear, recognizable feedback about
success or failure, in the primary modality of the content. Multi-step completion is marked (e.g., a
checkmark on finished steps). Confirmation persists long enough to be noticed and does not rely on
memory of a momentary flash.

### Visual scanning and layout comprehension _(from shared)_

When visual scanning is available and preferred, page regions such as the header, navigation, main
content, sidebar, and footer can help establish layout. Visible control affordances and hierarchy
through headings, spacing, colour, and size may help locate actions. Do not assume that visual
scanning is available or that these cues work alone.

**AT Mechanisms**: Visual perception **Device context**: All

**UI Requirements**: Clear visual hierarchy through headings, spacing, and grouping. Interactive
elements must look interactive (visible borders, underlines, or other affordances). Primary actions
should be visually prominent.

### Mouse / pointer interaction _(from shared)_

Mouse, trackpad, or touch-pointer interaction may involve clicking, selecting text, dragging,
hovering for tooltips, or opening context menus. Evaluate the actions supported by the chosen input
method; hover and sustained dragging are not available in every pointer context.

**AT Mechanisms**: Mouse, Trackpad, Touchscreen **Device context**: Desktop

**UI Requirements**: Interactive elements are easy to activate with the intended pointer action and
are far enough apart that users do not accidentally activate adjacent controls. Hover states should
indicate interactivity. Drag-and-drop should have clear affordances.

### Reading and scrolling _(from shared)_

Visual reading may proceed sequentially or through heading scans, with scrolling to reach content
beyond the viewport. Evaluate logical flow appropriate to the writing system, such as top-to-bottom
and left-to-right in LTR content. Do not assume one reading direction or scrolling method across
languages and input configurations.

**AT Mechanisms**: Visual reading, Mouse wheel / scroll gesture **Device context**: All

**UI Requirements**: Text must be readable: sufficient contrast, reasonable font size, adequate line
spacing. Content should not require horizontal scrolling at standard viewport widths. Long pages
should provide navigation aids (table of contents, back-to-top).

### Form interaction _(from shared)_

Form tasks may involve reaching fields, typing, selecting options, checking boxes, and submitting
through different input methods. Evaluate clear labels, placeholder text that does not replace
labels, and actionable error feedback without assuming pointer access.

**AT Mechanisms**: Keyboard (typing), Mouse (clicking fields), Visual reading (labels) **Device
context**: All

**UI Requirements**: Every form field must have a visible label. Required fields must be clearly
indicated. Error messages must be specific, adjacent to the field, and visible without scrolling.
Successful submission must be clearly confirmed.

### Navigation and wayfinding _(from shared)_

Menus, breadcrumbs, links, and browser back/forward can support navigation between pages and views.
Evaluate whether the current location, available destinations, and return paths are understandable
through the access strategies in scope.

**AT Mechanisms**: Visual scanning, Mouse clicking, Browser controls **Device context**: All

**UI Requirements**: Consistent navigation across pages. Current location clearly indicated (active
nav item, breadcrumbs). Important actions reachable from any page. Browser back button should work
predictably.

### Touch / pointer interaction _(from shared)_

Touch interaction may include taps, swipes, pinch-zoom, or long presses, depending on the device and
access method. Direct touch has no hover preview; evaluate discoverable controls, target size, and
alternatives to complex gestures. Do not require every touch interaction pattern to be available to
the same person.

**AT Mechanisms**: Touchscreen tap, Swipe gestures, Pinch-zoom **Device context**: Mobile

**UI Requirements**: Touch targets are easy to activate without accidentally activating adjacent
controls — small or tightly packed targets force users to retry or hit the wrong control. No
reliance on hover for essential information. Custom gestures must have single-pointer alternatives.
Drag-and-drop must have tap-based alternatives. No accidental activation from scroll gestures.

## Interaction Contexts

### Reduced working-memory-demand contexts

Evaluate one to three items held at once as an illustration, not a fixed cognitive ceiling. Carrying
verification codes or choices across screens, transcription, and re-entry after Back may increase
error risk. Test autofill, password managers, and data carried forward; working-memory requirements
and available supports vary.

**Primary AT**: Form autofill, Password managers, Reminder tools **Device context**: All **Preferred
navigation**: Chunked task progression, Offloading to external aids, Feedback-confirmed action

**Expectations**:

- Nothing essential must be remembered from one step to the next; each step provides what it needs
- Codes, choices, and entered data persist and are visible at the point of use
- Authentication does not depend on recalling or transcribing strings
- Going back never deletes already-entered work

### Distraction and task re-entry contexts

Evaluate interruptions, notifications, autoplay, and movement or layout shifts that may disrupt task
context. A calmer presentation, re-entry signposts, reader mode, or focus mode may support
resumption. Sensitivity, preferences, and tools vary; distraction does not establish diagnosis or
overall attention capability.

**Primary AT**: Reading / focus extensions, OS focus modes **Device context**: All **Preferred
navigation**: Distraction management, Orientation and re-orientation, Recognition over recall
navigation

**Expectations**:

- Interruptions, animations, and notifications can be paused, dismissed, or turned off
- Content does not move, autoplay, or shift without the user initiating it
- Headings and breadcrumbs make it easy to re-orient after losing focus
- The critical path is short, with non-essential content kept out of the way

### Planning, sequencing, and processing-time contexts

Evaluate planning, organization, sequencing, and additional processing or decision-time
requirements. Up-front steps, expected time, required materials, and visible progress may help.
Pressure, hidden complexity, and tight limits can block a flow even when individual steps are
operable.

**Primary AT**: Reminder / task tools, Read-aloud, Personalization **Device context**: All
**Preferred navigation**: Chunked task progression, Orientation and re-orientation, Offloading to
external aids

**Expectations**:

- The task's steps, expected time, and required materials are stated before it begins
- Steps are clearly separated and ordered, with visible progress
- There is enough time; timeouts are avoided or generously extendable, with no data loss
- Optional and promotional steps are separated from the required path

### Stable-interface and relearning contexts

Evaluate familiar conventions where new patterns are difficult to learn or retain, including
progressive-memory-decline research contexts. New terms, relocated controls, and redesigned flows
may remove a previously usable path. Check user- or caregiver-configured simplification, stable
setups, and options to retain or roll back to a familiar version without predicting learning ability
from diagnosis.

**Primary AT**: Personalization (familiar interface), caregiver-configured simplification **Device
context**: All **Preferred navigation**: Recognition over recall navigation, Orientation and
re-orientation, Feedback-confirmed action

**Expectations**:

- Familiar, conventional patterns and terms — nothing new to learn to do routine tasks
- Controls and labels stay where and what they were; changes are not forced
- A simpler, stable presentation is available and can be made the default
- Common words and consistent labels for every concept across the whole experience

### New / infrequent user _(from shared)_

First-time or infrequent interaction may require rediscovering controls and workflows. Labels,
accessible affordances, and progressive disclosure can support learning without requiring prior
knowledge of shortcuts or hidden features.

**Primary AT**: None **Device context**: All **Preferred navigation**: Visual scanning and layout
comprehension, Navigation and wayfinding, Form interaction

**Expectations**:

- Interface is learnable without documentation
- Primary actions are visually obvious
- Error messages guide toward resolution
- Navigation structure is understandable

### Expert / frequent user _(from shared)_

Frequent interaction may build familiarity with layouts, shortcuts, and workflows. Consistency and
efficient paths can support established strategies; changes may require relearning. Expertise in a
task does not establish expertise with every assistive technology.

**Primary AT**: None **Device context**: All **Preferred navigation**: Mouse / pointer interaction,
Navigation and wayfinding

**Expectations**:

- Keyboard shortcuts for common tasks
- Consistent layout across sessions and pages
- Efficient paths to frequently used features
- Changes to familiar workflows are clearly communicated

## Learning Considerations

- **Relearning cost may recur**: Unfamiliar controls may require relearning on repeated encounters
  or remain inaccessible in some contexts, including progressive decline. Evaluate familiar
  conventions and options to retain a learned interface without assigning universal learning
  capacity.

- **Working-memory demands can be barriers**: Remembered values, vanished instructions, and
  transcribed codes may introduce failure points. Evaluate information available at the point of use
  and support for carrying it forward without declaring working memory a fixed ceiling.

- **Distraction and re-entry**: Interruptions can make resumption difficult. Evaluate headings,
  breadcrumbs, and preserved drafts as re-entry signposts; missing context may slow or prevent
  completion.

- **Fatigue may accumulate**: Cognitive effort and fatigue vary over a session, and errors or
  abandonment may increase. A previously manageable task may become difficult or unavailable.
  Evaluate breaks, resumption, and avoidance of timeouts or data loss.

- **Confidence and self-blame**: Repeated errors and confusing recoveries erode the user's
  confidence and often lead them to blame themselves and stop using the service. Forgiving, clearly
  recoverable interactions and reassuring feedback protect not just the current task but the user's
  willingness to return.

- **First visit** _(from shared)_: Familiar web, desktop, and mobile conventions may support
  learning, but recognition of hamburger menus, search icons, tabs, or modals is not guaranteed.
  Explain unfamiliar patterns and expose their purpose through accessible labels and feedback.

- **Mental models** _(from shared)_: Early interactions may establish expectations. Inconsistent
  behavior, such as a button that sometimes navigates and sometimes submits, can undermine
  confidence and require additional checking.

- **Error recovery** _(from shared)_: Support recovery from mistakes through undo, appropriate
  confirmation dialogs, and clear error messages. Make the consequences of irreversible actions
  clear rather than assuming every mistake can be avoided.

- **Cognitive load** _(from shared)_: Clutter, excessive options, and unclear hierarchy can increase
  attention and memory demands. Evaluate those demands in the task context without inferring a
  person's cognitive capabilities.

## Heuristic Lens Overrides

### Heuristic 1 — Visibility of System Status

Evaluate immediate, explicit, persistent confirmation through messages, changed states, or
completed-step checkmarks. Momentary flashes may be missed. Show done, current, and remaining work
for task re-entry. Silent success, such as an email "sent" without acknowledgement, can cause
uncertainty or repetition where completion is difficult to recall.

### Heuristic 2 — Match Between System and Real World

Familiar terms and patterns can reduce learning and recall demands; novel metaphors or coined words
may add barriers. Use plain literal instructions, labels, and actions. Apply the
audience-appropriate-language principle from Evaluation Considerations: necessary audience-familiar
domain terms (an admin console's resource names, a scientific tool's parameters) are not defects,
but require consistent naming, persistent labels, and on-demand definitions. Flag gratuitous jargon,
unexplained acronyms, and inconsistent naming, not necessary consistently used domain vocabulary.

### Heuristic 3 — User Control and Freedom

Evaluate predictable Back and undo without discarding entered work. Clearing a form may impose
substantial re-entry effort or block completion. Avoid uninitiated context changes, new windows,
rerouting, or submission. Provide clearly labeled routes back, reopening, and cancellation.

### Heuristic 4 — Consistency and Standards

Evaluate consistent controls, terminology, placement, and behavior so recognition can transfer
across screens. Moving controls, changed terms, or buttons that alternate submission and navigation
may force relearning and disrupt a learned model. Prefer platform and design-system conventions
while checking familiarity rather than assuming prior knowledge.

### Heuristic 5 — Error Prevention

Preventing errors matters more than helping recover from them, because recovery itself taxes memory
and attention. Remove memory-driven failure modes: autofill routine data, accept multiple input
formats, never require a remembered code carried across steps, and offer authentication that does
not depend on recall. Confirm destructive actions, but place the confirmation and its consequences
in plain view. State fees, requirements, and what the user will need before a task begins, and avoid
timeouts that punish a user who pauses to look something up.

### Heuristic 6 — Recognition Rather than Recall

Evaluate information required to act at the point of use: field instructions, carried-forward
choices, and current-step context. Labels and instructions must not vanish on focus. Avoid required
recall of codes, selections, menu locations, or shortcuts; show information in the step where it is
needed.

### Heuristic 7 — Flexibility and Efficiency of Use

Efficiency here means fewer things to track, not power-user acceleration. Keep critical paths short
and required steps minimal; separate optional and promotional steps out of the path. Offer
accelerators that offload memory — add-to-calendar, autosave and resume, recently-used and saved
items, autofill — rather than shortcuts the user must learn and remember. Never make efficiency
contingent on memorized commands or gestures.

### Heuristic 8 — Aesthetic and Minimalist Design

Clutter and excess choice may contribute to overload, lost focus, or abandonment. Evaluate clear
prioritized choices, white space, and secondary content outside the main path. Reduce competing
animations, autoplay, and noisy backgrounds without removing headings, step indicators, or landmarks
needed for orientation.

### Heuristic 9 — Help Users Recognize, Diagnose, and Recover from Errors

When errors happen, the message must be in plain language, located at the point of the error, and
explicit about how to fix it — not a generic code or a summary the user must map back to a field.
Critically, entered data must be preserved through the correction so the user does not have to redo
work (and re-introduce errors). Let the user review and change earlier entries and return to where
they were in one clearly labeled step, without losing other data.

### Heuristic 10 — Help and Documentation

Help must be reachable from any point where the user might get stuck, in as few steps as possible,
and must include a path to a human that is not buried behind a complex menu or voice-response tree.
Provide step-by-step instructions with concrete examples, and tell the user up front what a task
involves — the steps, the time, and the materials needed — so they can prepare. Help content itself
must follow the same plain-language and recognition principles, or it becomes another barrier.
