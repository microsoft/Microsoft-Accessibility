# FPS 4.2.10 — Usage with limited cognition, language or learning (language and communication)

The ICT provides features and/or presentation that makes it simpler and easier to understand,
operate, and use.

## Evaluation Considerations

Evaluate possible barriers to reading, decoding, understanding, and producing language, and the
supports needed to access content and complete tasks. These requirements vary and may overlap with
sensory, motor, memory, attention, and numeracy requirements. Do not simulate a person, assign a
diagnosis, or assume unrelated capabilities are unaffected.

Assess uncommon words, acronyms, jargon, complex or passive sentences, double negatives, and nested
clauses relative to audience familiarity and available explanations. Literal interpretation may make
metaphors, idioms, sarcasm, or implied meaning unclear. Reading may involve sounding out one or two
words at a time, with possible fatigue; some contexts use text-to-speech, familiar symbols, or AAC
(augmentative and alternative communication) for comprehension or expression. Dense text, images of
text, and busy backgrounds can defeat those supports. None of these patterns describes every person
with a language or learning disability.

For each screen or flow, consider:

- **READ**: Is text presented so it can be decoded — clear readable fonts, adequate spacing, short
  left-justified lines, and not overlaid on busy or patterned backgrounds?
- **VOCABULARY**: Are common, everyday words used? Where uncommon words, acronyms, or jargon appear,
  are they avoided where possible or explained within one click?
- **SYNTAX**: Are sentences short, active, and about one thing each? Are double negatives and nested
  clauses avoided?
- **LITERAL**: Is meaning explicit, with no unexplained metaphors, idioms, sarcasm, or implied
  content the user must infer?
- **STRUCTURE**: Is content chunked with clear headings, short paragraphs, lists, summaries, and
  white space — or is it a dense block?
- **SYMBOLS / ICONS**: Do important controls and section headings carry familiar supporting icons or
  symbols? Is meaning conveyed by more than text alone where a symbol would help?
- **ALTERNATIVES**: Are there alternatives to dense text — a plain-language summary, images,
  diagrams, video, or read-aloud — and do reading and simplification extensions work on the page?
- **SPEECH OUTPUT**: Is content real, well-ordered text with correct language tags and punctuation,
  so text-to-speech and phonetic readers pronounce and chunk it correctly?

Possible access barriers include: long, dense text with no summary; uncommon words, jargon, and
unexplained acronyms; non-literal language; complex or passive sentences; text presented as images
that text-to-speech cannot read; labels and instructions that disappear; icon-only controls with no
text where the symbol is unfamiliar, or text-only labels where a symbol would aid comprehension;
words on busy backgrounds; and content that breaks reading or simplification extensions.

IMPORTANT — Audience-appropriate language and terminology:

This is the consideration for which "clear language" matters most directly — and it is exactly where
evaluators most often over-flag. Clarity is **relative to the actual user base and domain**, not
measured against a universal "simplest possible words" floor. Necessary domain terminology that the
intended users know and need is not a clarity failure; gratuitous or unsupported complexity is.

Calibrate as follows. An administration console for site-reliability engineers, or a
mass-spectrometry analysis tool for chemists, legitimately uses precise domain terms (`namespace`,
`ingress`, `m/z ratio`, `retention time`) that its users rely on; demanding the "most common 1500
words" in that context would make the product wrong, not clearer. So do not flag domain-correct
vocabulary that the audience knows and needs.

Do flag, regardless of domain: acronyms and jargon used **without** a one-click definition; coined
words or familiar words given new meanings; non-literal language (metaphor, idiom, sarcasm)
presented without explanation; needlessly complex or passive sentence construction where a simple
one would serve; the same concept named inconsistently across the interface; and necessary domain
terms left **unsupported** — no glossary, no on-hover definition, no example, no supporting symbol
for users still learning them. The test is twofold: is the terminology (a) appropriate to and known
by the intended audience, and (b) scaffolded for those still acquiring it — not whether it meets a
universal simplicity target.

## Assistive Technologies

Evaluate compatibility with tools for decoding, comprehension, and expression, including familiar
symbols and mainstream reading features. Tool use varies, and some contexts have no configured AT.
Expose real text and avoid disabling these supports.

### Text-to-speech / read-aloud with synchronized highlighting

**Category**: read-aloud **Platforms**: Windows, macOS, iOS, Android, browser extensions **Device
context**: All

Read-aloud tools speak on-screen text, often highlighting each word or phrase. This can be a primary
comprehension route or a complementary support. Test real, selectable text, logical reading order,
correct language tags, and sensible punctuation; images of text, scrambled DOM order, or missing
language attributes can cause skips or mispronunciation that change meaning.

### Reading and simplification toolbars

**Category**: reading-support **Platforms**: Browser extensions, reading apps (desktop and mobile)
**Device context**: Desktop, Mobile

Reading toolbars adjust font, spacing, and line length, add a reading ruler, mask distractions,
generate summaries, or simplify wording. Evaluate whether dense content can be reshaped while
preserving meaning. Canvas, images, and custom widgets that cannot be reached or reflowed can defeat
this support.

### Symbol and AAC systems

**Category**: symbol-aac **Platforms**: Dedicated AAC devices, iOS, Android, browser/extension
symbol sets **Device context**: All

Symbol sets and AAC systems support comprehension through familiar pictographic or ideographic
symbols and expression through symbol selection. Familiarity and known vocabularies vary; learning
an unfamiliar symbol set may present a barrier. Personalization semantics can load a preferred known
symbol set. Evaluate word-only and unfamiliar-icon paths without assuming that AAC use establishes
reading ability or intellectual disability.

### Spell-check, grammar, and word-prediction tools

**Category**: writing-support **Platforms**: OS, browser, extensions, AAC apps **Device context**:
All

Prediction, spelling, and grammar tools can reduce spelling and recall demands and support field
completion where unaided spelling is difficult. Test standard editable fields and avoid blocking or
overriding the tool, including suppressing right-click or rejecting paste.

### Dictionary and glossary pop-ups

**Category**: definition-tool **Platforms**: Browser extensions, in-page glossaries **Device
context**: All

On-demand definition tools let the user select or hover a word and see a plain-language meaning, so
an uncommon or domain term becomes usable rather than a dead end. They depend on words being real,
selectable text and benefit from the interface providing its own in-page definitions or glossary for
terms specific to the domain.

### Translation and phonetic readers

**Category**: language-support **Platforms**: Browser, OS, extensions **Device context**: All

Translation tools and phonetic readers help users who do not know the interface's language well, or
who need diacritics and phonetic cues to decode words (as in languages like Arabic and Hebrew). They
depend on correct language tagging and on the necessary letters and marks being present in the text;
missing language attributes or stripped diacritics make the output wrong.

### Personalization semantics

**Category**: personalization **Platforms**: Browser, extensions **Device context**: All

Personalization mechanisms can load familiar symbols, attach easy-language explanations, or select a
simplified version through exposed semantics. Preferences may be configured once by the user or a
chosen helper and reused across sites. Evaluate adaptation hooks and simpler alternatives that
preserve meaning and the critical path.

## Exploration Strategies

### Decoding-supported reading

Evaluate reading with text-to-speech highlighting, a reading ruler, extra spacing, or slower
sounding-out. Check real text, logical order, clean punctuation, and language tags. Images of text,
disordered content, or busy backgrounds may make these supports fail; assisted reading does not
establish a person's fluency in other contexts.

**AT Mechanisms**: Read-aloud, Reading toolbars, Phonetic readers **Device context**: All

**UI Requirements**: Provide real, selectable text in a logical reading order with correct language
tags. Use clear fonts, generous spacing, and short left-justified lines. Do not overlay text on busy
or low-contrast backgrounds. Never present essential text only as an image.

### Skim-for-structure reading

Evaluate gist-first reading using headings, summaries, bold key points, short lists, and supporting
icons. Where word-by-word reading is slow or tiring, dense unstructured prose can increase effort or
hide important information. Check whether necessary details remain findable without reading every
word.

**AT Mechanisms**: Visual scanning, Reading toolbars (summaries) **Device context**: All

**UI Requirements**: Provide a clear heading structure that outlines the content, plain-language
summaries for long material, short chunked paragraphs and lists, and emphasis on key points. Put the
most important information first. Do not bury critical content in dense prose.

### Symbol- and icon-led navigation

Evaluate familiar-symbol paths for help, search, home, contact, headings, and links where symbols
support comprehension more readily than text. Familiarity varies; conventional and personalizable
symbols may help, while unfamiliar bespoke icons or text-only controls may present barriers.

**AT Mechanisms**: Visual scanning, Symbol/AAC systems, Personalization **Device context**: All

**UI Requirements**: Add familiar, conventional icons or symbols to important controls and section
headings, placed next to the content they label. Pair icons with text. Support personalization so
users can load symbols they already know. Avoid relying on unfamiliar icons alone.

### Literal interpretation

Evaluate instructions and labels under literal interpretation. Metaphors, idioms, sarcasm, unstated
context, and implied steps can produce misunderstandings where figurative inference is difficult.
Check explicit concrete wording and explanations attached to non-literal content without assigning
that interpretation to a whole community.

**AT Mechanisms**: Visual reading, Read-aloud **Device context**: All

**UI Requirements**: Use literal, concrete language for anything that matters. Explain or avoid
metaphors, idioms, sarcasm, and implied content. Make instructions explicit and unambiguous — do not
assume the user will infer steps, consequences, or context that is not stated.

### Alternative-format substitution

Test switching from difficult or dense text to a plain-language summary, simpler version, image,
diagram, short video, or read-aloud. Alternatives must be findable, equivalent in meaning, and
clearly related to the primary content. Suitability varies; do not assume that one alternative works
for everyone.

**AT Mechanisms**: Read-aloud, Reading toolbars, Personalization **Device context**: All

**UI Requirements**: Offer easy-to-find alternatives for complex or long content — summaries,
simplified versions, images, diagrams, video, and read-aloud — that convey the same information and
are clearly linked to the primary content. Provide a simple, single action to switch to the format
that is easiest for the user.

### Assisted expression

Evaluate expression with word prediction, spell/grammar support, symbols that speak words, or speech
input. Dictation or prediction may support responses where unaided writing is difficult. Fields that
block tools, demand exact spelling unnecessarily, or reject supported non-text input can remove a
usable response path.

**AT Mechanisms**: Word-prediction, Spell/grammar tools, Symbol/AAC, Speech input **Device
context**: All

**UI Requirements**: Use standard editable fields that work with prediction, spelling, and speech
tools. Do not block paste or the right-click menu. Minimize the amount of free text the user must
produce; accept forgiving input and offer selection over spelling where possible.

### Visual scanning and layout comprehension _(from shared)_

When visual scanning is available and preferred, page regions such as the header, navigation, main
content, sidebar, and footer can help establish layout. Visible control affordances and hierarchy
through headings, spacing, colour, and size may help locate actions. Do not assume that visual
scanning is available or that these cues work alone.

**AT Mechanisms**: Visual perception **Device context**: All

**UI Requirements**: Clear visual hierarchy through headings, spacing, and grouping. Interactive
elements must look interactive (visible borders, underlines, or other affordances). Primary actions
should be visually prominent.

### Mouse / pointer interaction _(from shared)_

Mouse, trackpad, or touch-pointer interaction may involve clicking, selecting text, dragging,
hovering for tooltips, or opening context menus. Evaluate the actions supported by the chosen input
method; hover and sustained dragging are not available in every pointer context.

**AT Mechanisms**: Mouse, Trackpad, Touchscreen **Device context**: Desktop

**UI Requirements**: Interactive elements are easy to activate with the intended pointer action and
are far enough apart that users do not accidentally activate adjacent controls. Hover states should
indicate interactivity. Drag-and-drop should have clear affordances.

### Reading and scrolling _(from shared)_

Visual reading may proceed sequentially or through heading scans, with scrolling to reach content
beyond the viewport. Evaluate logical flow appropriate to the writing system, such as top-to-bottom
and left-to-right in LTR content. Do not assume one reading direction or scrolling method across
languages and input configurations.

**AT Mechanisms**: Visual reading, Mouse wheel / scroll gesture **Device context**: All

**UI Requirements**: Text must be readable: sufficient contrast, reasonable font size, adequate line
spacing. Content should not require horizontal scrolling at standard viewport widths. Long pages
should provide navigation aids (table of contents, back-to-top).

### Form interaction _(from shared)_

Form tasks may involve reaching fields, typing, selecting options, checking boxes, and submitting
through different input methods. Evaluate clear labels, placeholder text that does not replace
labels, and actionable error feedback without assuming pointer access.

**AT Mechanisms**: Keyboard (typing), Mouse (clicking fields), Visual reading (labels) **Device
context**: All

**UI Requirements**: Every form field must have a visible label. Required fields must be clearly
indicated. Error messages must be specific, adjacent to the field, and visible without scrolling.
Successful submission must be clearly confirmed.

### Navigation and wayfinding _(from shared)_

Menus, breadcrumbs, links, and browser back/forward can support navigation between pages and views.
Evaluate whether the current location, available destinations, and return paths are understandable
through the access strategies in scope.

**AT Mechanisms**: Visual scanning, Mouse clicking, Browser controls **Device context**: All

**UI Requirements**: Consistent navigation across pages. Current location clearly indicated (active
nav item, breadcrumbs). Important actions reachable from any page. Browser back button should work
predictably.

### Touch / pointer interaction _(from shared)_

Touch interaction may include taps, swipes, pinch-zoom, or long presses, depending on the device and
access method. Direct touch has no hover preview; evaluate discoverable controls, target size, and
alternatives to complex gestures. Do not require every touch interaction pattern to be available to
the same person.

**AT Mechanisms**: Touchscreen tap, Swipe gestures, Pinch-zoom **Device context**: Mobile

**UI Requirements**: Touch targets are easy to activate without accidentally activating adjacent
controls — small or tightly packed targets force users to retry or hit the wrong control. No
reliance on hover for essential information. Custom gestures must have single-pointer alternatives.
Drag-and-drop must have tap-based alternatives. No accidental activation from scroll gestures.

## Interaction Contexts

### Decoding and reading-support contexts

Evaluate slower or effortful decoding, which may include sounding out one or two words at a time and
fatigue. Dyslexia research discusses varied reading requirements; it does not define one
representative pattern or establish intelligence or comprehension in other contexts. Check
text-to-speech, clear fonts, generous spacing, short lines, summaries, and the visibility of
information in dense prose.

**Primary AT**: Read-aloud, Reading toolbars **Device context**: All **Preferred navigation**:
Decoding-supported reading, Skim-for-structure reading, Alternative-format substitution

**Expectations**:

- Real, selectable text that read-aloud can voice, in a logical order
- Clear fonts, generous spacing, short left-justified lines, no text on busy backgrounds
- Plain-language summaries and clear headings so key points are findable without reading everything
- Important information first, not buried mid-page

### Language comprehension and expression contexts

Evaluate comprehension and expression requirements that may occur, for example, with aphasia after a
stroke. Short literal text and read-aloud may help; complex words, passive constructions, and long
sentences may be difficult or fatiguing. Check copying of required wording where unaided production
is unavailable, and preservation of entered data where re-entry is slow or error-prone.

**Primary AT**: Read-aloud, Word-prediction, Reading toolbars **Device context**: All **Preferred
navigation**: Decoding-supported reading, Literal interpretation, Assisted expression

**Expectations**:

- Short sentences, active voice, common words, one idea at a time
- Read-aloud support with clear, well-punctuated text
- Forgiving input that accepts assisted and approximate expression; no loss of entered text
- Clear, literal instructions with examples

### Familiar-symbol and AAC-supported contexts

Evaluate contexts using familiar pictographic symbols or AAC for comprehension and communication.
These supports may be relevant in intellectual-disability research, but their use does not establish
diagnosis or reading and writing capability. Text-heavy paths and unfamiliar symbol vocabularies may
be barriers; check recognizable symbols beside controls and headings and support for a preferred
known symbol set.

**Primary AT**: Symbol/AAC systems, Personalization **Device context**: All **Preferred
navigation**: Symbol- and icon-led navigation, Alternative-format substitution, Assisted expression

**Expectations**:

- Familiar, conventional symbols and icons on important controls and headings, paired with text
- Personalization support to load the user's own known symbol set
- Word-free or symbol-based paths for key actions, including login and help
- Alternatives to blocks of text — images, symbols, short video

### Literal-language interpretation contexts

Evaluate literal or concrete interpretation of metaphors, idioms, sarcasm, jokes, and implied or
ambiguous content, including abstract imagery with unstated meaning. Some autistic people may
describe such requirements, with substantial individual variation; this is not a community profile
or evidence of intellectual capability. Check explicit wording, explanations, and logical structure.

**Primary AT**: Read-aloud, Definition tools **Device context**: All **Preferred navigation**:
Literal interpretation, Skim-for-structure reading, Navigation and wayfinding

**Expectations**:

- Literal, concrete language; metaphors, idioms, and sarcasm explained or avoided
- Explicit instructions and clearly stated consequences — nothing left to inference
- Consistent, logical structure and navigation
- Explanations available for non-literal or ambiguous content, including in images and media

### New / infrequent user _(from shared)_

First-time or infrequent interaction may require rediscovering controls and workflows. Labels,
accessible affordances, and progressive disclosure can support learning without requiring prior
knowledge of shortcuts or hidden features.

**Primary AT**: None **Device context**: All **Preferred navigation**: Visual scanning and layout
comprehension, Navigation and wayfinding, Form interaction

**Expectations**:

- Interface is learnable without documentation
- Primary actions are visually obvious
- Error messages guide toward resolution
- Navigation structure is understandable

### Expert / frequent user _(from shared)_

Frequent interaction may build familiarity with layouts, shortcuts, and workflows. Consistency and
efficient paths can support established strategies; changes may require relearning. Expertise in a
task does not establish expertise with every assistive technology.

**Primary AT**: None **Device context**: All **Preferred navigation**: Mouse / pointer interaction,
Navigation and wayfinding

**Expectations**:

- Keyboard shortcuts for common tasks
- Consistent layout across sessions and pages
- Efficient paths to frequently used features
- Changes to familiar workflows are clearly communicated

## Learning Considerations

- **Vocabulary acquisition varies**: New terms may be effortful to acquire or may remain
  inaccessible in some contexts, including significant language impairment. Coined words,
  unexplained acronyms, and jargon without a one-click definition can make content unusable.
  Evaluate audience-appropriate common words and on-demand explanations without predicting anyone's
  learning ceiling.

- **Reading effort and fatigue vary**: Long or dense text may exhaust available effort before the
  key point is reached. Evaluate front-loaded messages, structured chunks, and summaries that
  preserve information while reducing reading burden.

- **Symbols must be familiar to help**: Known symbols can reduce language load; unfamiliar sets may
  add substantial learning or decoding effort. Evaluate familiar, conventional, and personalizable
  symbols without assuming any symbol is universally understood.

- **Literal interpretation may need support**: Implied, figurative, or ambiguous meaning may be
  missed without awareness of misunderstanding. Evaluate explicit literal wording and attached
  explanations to reduce misleading interpretations.

- **Speech output depends on clean text**: Text-to-speech and phonetic readers are only as good as
  the markup beneath them. Images of text, illogical DOM order, missing language tags, and unusual
  punctuation cause skips and mispronunciations that change or lose the meaning. Clean,
  well-ordered, well-tagged text is what makes the user's primary support reliable.

- **First visit** _(from shared)_: Familiar web, desktop, and mobile conventions may support
  learning, but recognition of hamburger menus, search icons, tabs, or modals is not guaranteed.
  Explain unfamiliar patterns and expose their purpose through accessible labels and feedback.

- **Mental models** _(from shared)_: Early interactions may establish expectations. Inconsistent
  behavior, such as a button that sometimes navigates and sometimes submits, can undermine
  confidence and require additional checking.

- **Error recovery** _(from shared)_: Support recovery from mistakes through undo, appropriate
  confirmation dialogs, and clear error messages. Make the consequences of irreversible actions
  clear rather than assuming every mistake can be avoided.

- **Cognitive load** _(from shared)_: Clutter, excessive options, and unclear hierarchy can increase
  attention and memory demands. Evaluate those demands in the task context without inferring a
  person's cognitive capabilities.

## Heuristic Lens Overrides

### Heuristic 1 — Visibility of System Status

Evaluate readable status, not merely its presence: plain literal text, familiar supporting symbols,
and read-aloud-friendly content where useful. Rapid explicit feedback for "send" or "save" can
reduce uncertainty when decoding is slower; dense status messages may conceal success or failure.

### Heuristic 2 — Match Between System and Real World

This is the central heuristic for this consideration. Use common, everyday words, simple active
sentences, and literal language; explain or avoid metaphor, idiom, and sarcasm; and pair important
controls and headings with familiar real-world symbols. Apply the audience-appropriate-language
principle from Evaluation Considerations in full: domain terminology the actual user base knows and
needs is acceptable and should not be flagged, but it must be used consistently and **supported**
with on-demand definitions, examples, or symbols. Flag gratuitous jargon, unexplained acronyms,
coined or repurposed words, non-literal language without explanation, the same concept named
differently across screens, and necessary domain terms left unsupported. Do not flag domain-correct
vocabulary that an expert audience relies on.

### Heuristic 3 — User Control and Freedom

Provide exits, undo, and key actions that do not depend on producing or reading a lot of language. A
user who cannot easily spell or read should still be able to cancel, go back, log in, or get help —
through symbols, simple single-step actions, or assisted input — without being trapped in a
text-heavy flow. Make actions reversible and never punish the user for input they could not produce
perfectly.

### Heuristic 4 — Consistency and Standards

Evaluate one consistent word per concept and consistent icon or symbol meanings. Shifting
terminology or symbol meaning may require renewed decoding. Conventional, stable labels can support
recognition of learned content; familiarity and re-decoding effort vary.

### Heuristic 5 — Error Prevention

Evaluate clear audience-appropriate labels, examples, forgiving formats, and instructions before
fields rather than only after errors. Minimize unnecessary free-text production and offer selection
over spelling where possible. Literal, example-led instructions may reduce errors from confusing or
jargon-laden prompts.

### Heuristic 6 — Recognition Rather than Recall

Evaluate recognizable meaning through persistent audience-appropriate labels, familiar symbols, and
one-click definitions. Essential meaning should not depend on fluent decoding of dense text or
knowledge of unfamiliar icons. A definition's presence does not alone establish comprehension; check
its clarity and relationship to the task.

### Heuristic 7 — Flexibility and Efficiency of Use

Evaluate equivalent plain-language summaries, simplified versions, images, diagrams, short video,
and read-aloud, with persistent personalization where possible. Let the user choose a suitable
format. Symbols, definitions, and assisted input can reduce reading and writing load; text shortcuts
alone do not address these requirements.

### Heuristic 8 — Aesthetic and Minimalist Design

Reduce the reading load. Use short chunks, one idea per paragraph, lists, generous white space, and
summaries, and keep foreground text off busy backgrounds so words can be recognized at a glance.
Extra words and decorative distractions may add decoding effort and fatigue. Minimalism should cut
text and clutter while keeping the headings, summaries, and supporting symbols that make meaning
findable.

### Heuristic 9 — Help Users Recognize, Diagnose, and Recover from Errors

Write error messages in plain, literal language that says what went wrong and exactly how to fix it,
with an example — not a jargon phrase, an error code, or a vague "invalid input". Reinforce the
message with an icon and keep it next to the field it concerns. Where corrected input requires
substantial effort, preserve entered text and keep correction small and guided.

### Heuristic 10 — Help and Documentation

Help must be written in clear, common language with supporting symbols and concrete examples, and
must offer alternatives to dense text — summaries, images, video, and an easy route to a human.
Definitions for domain terms should be available on demand within the help itself. Help that is as
dense and jargon-laden as the content it explains is no help at all; it has to model the same
plain-language and symbol support the rest of the interface should provide.
