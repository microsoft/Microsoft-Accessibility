# FPS 4.2.5 — Usage with limited hearing

Where ICT provides auditory modes of operation, the ICT provides enhanced audio features.

## Evaluation Considerations

Evaluate whether enhanced audio and visual supplements support reliable access when audio is only
partly perceivable. Check quiet sounds, speech against noise, high-frequency consonants/alerts, and
asymmetric audio access. These are possible interaction conditions, not a portrayal of a person with
limited hearing or a claim that Deaf people uniformly perceive no sound.

Relevant contexts may range from mild hearing loss (soft speech or volume barriers) to moderately
severe hearing loss, with hearing aids, cochlear implants, or caption support as applicable. Device
use and caption needs cannot be inferred from severity. Also assess noisy environments, one earbud
in, and poor call audio quality.

Assess EACH context without assuming consistent outcomes:

1. Assistive hearing devices (hearing aids or cochlear implant), where processed audio may support
   speech in quiet but noise, poor quality, or overlapping speakers may reduce intelligibility
2. No assistive hearing device, where volume controls, enhanced audio settings, mono audio, and
   captions may provide important support

For each screenshot or interface, consider:

- Does the interface offer VOLUME CONTROLS that go beyond the system default? Can audio be boosted?
- Is there BACKGROUND NOISE in audio content? Can speech be separated or enhanced relative to
  background sound?
- Are CAPTIONS available alongside enhanced audio? Check supplemental reading for missed words;
  captions may become the primary channel when listening conditions deteriorate.
- For STEREO AUDIO: is a mono audio option available? A user with single-sided hearing loss misses
  content panned to their impaired side.
- Are AUDIO ALERTS at frequencies and volumes that the user can perceive? High-frequency tones are
  commonly missed.
- For COMMUNICATION features: is audio quality sufficient? Can the user adjust call audio settings
  (volume, noise cancellation)?
- Are there VISUAL REDUNDANCIES for important audio information? Visual indicators alongside sounds
  provide a safety net when audio is partially missed.

Possible barriers include quiet cues without visual backup, speech mixed with noise or music,
high-frequency alerts outside the relevant hearing/device range, stereo-only audio without mono,
poor call quality, unexpected auto-play volume, and missing per-stream controls (e.g., reducing
music while keeping speech loud).

This lens assesses enhanced audio (volume boost, noise cancellation, frequency adjustment),
captions, and visual redundancy for reliable access. It does not require audio use when another
channel is preferable or assume unrelated sensory, motor, or cognitive capabilities. Preserve audio
enhancement as a distinct requirement from replacing audio entirely.

## Assistive Technologies

### Hearing aids and cochlear implants

**Category**: hearing_device **Platforms**: Cross-platform (Bluetooth pairing with Windows, macOS,
iOS, Android) **Device context**: All

Hearing aids amplify and shape sound to an audiogram, including frequency-specific boosting.
Bluetooth-capable devices can stream from phones/computers, bypassing room acoustics and ambient
noise; compatibility must be checked. Cochlear implants stimulate the auditory nerve electrically,
providing a representation of sound different from acoustic hearing. Speech intelligibility varies;
music, tonal nuances, and overlapping speakers may be harder to distinguish. Both benefit from
clean, separated signals; noise, compression artifacts, and poor microphones can degrade them.
Telecoil (T-coil) connects compatible hearing aids to venue hearing loops and is less relevant to
screen-based evaluation.

### Mono audio output

**Category**: audio_setting **Platforms**: Windows, macOS, iOS, Android **Device context**: All

System setting combining stereo channels into mono so both channels' content is available through
one output. Relevant where single-sided (unilateral) or asymmetric hearing loss, or one-earbud use,
makes one channel unavailable; it does not guarantee perception of every sound. Panned effects,
music, or spatially separated speech may otherwise be partly or completely missed. Most operating
systems provide a mono toggle in accessibility settings.

### Caption systems (supplementary)

**Category**: captioning **Platforms**: Windows, macOS, iOS, Android, Web **Device context**: All

System-level live captions, browser captions, and platform meeting captions also used in the
audio-alternatives consideration. Here assess captions alongside partially heard audio for missed
words. They may be toggled as noise, audio quality, or accent familiarity changes, or become the
primary channel in difficult conditions; no group-wide pattern is assumed.

### Enhanced audio settings

**Category**: audio_setting **Platforms**: Windows, macOS, iOS, Android **Device context**: All

System and application-level audio enhancements: volume boost beyond normal maximum, equaliser
settings to emphasise speech frequencies, noise cancellation or reduction to separate speech from
background noise, audio compression to reduce dynamic range (making quiet sounds louder and loud
sounds softer). Windows provides Sound Enhancements and Loudness Equalization. macOS offers per-app
volume in some contexts. iOS and Android provide hearing-specific audio adjustments. Some video
conferencing platforms offer noise suppression settings that significantly improve speech clarity
for hearing device users.

## Exploration Strategies

### Checking audio quality and volume controls

Check clear, controllable audio: volume sliders, boost beyond the default maximum, equalisers, noise
cancellation, and call-level settings. Compression artifacts, echo, and low bitrate can degrade
intelligibility, including when processed or amplified through a hearing device.

**AT Mechanisms**: Hearing device processing, system volume controls, application audio settings
**Device context**: All

**UI Requirements**: Volume controls should be easily discoverable and adjustable. Audio output
should be clean and well-encoded. Voice/video call platforms should offer noise cancellation or
suppression. Per-stream volume controls (separate speech and background music) are highly
beneficial. Auto-play audio should respect system volume settings and not play at unexpectedly loud
or quiet levels.

### Looking for captions as supplement

When captions supplement listening, check availability, toggling, and positioning for quick access
to missed words. Captions that obscure important visual content force an avoidable trade-off.
Supplemental use in this lens does not prevent captions from becoming the primary channel.

**AT Mechanisms**: Visual reading (captions), hearing device processing (audio) **Device context**:
All

**UI Requirements**: Captions should be optional and toggleable — not forced when the user doesn't
need them. Caption positioning should be configurable or at least not obstruct critical content.
Caption styling should be readable (adequate contrast, reasonable font size). Speaker identification
in captions helps when the user can hear that someone is talking but can't identify who.

### Verifying visual redundancy for audio cues

Check visual backups for quiet alerts, high-frequency tones, soft notification chimes, subtle action
sounds, and meaningful ambient audio. Perception may vary by environment and device; paired badges,
banners, or icon changes prevent access from depending on an intermittently heard cue.

**AT Mechanisms**: Visual scanning, hearing device processing **Device context**: All

**UI Requirements**: Provide visual equivalents for audio indicators when auditory perception is
unreliable. Pair every important cue with visible feedback that does not require watching a single
fixed screen region.

### Checking for background noise separation

When speech mixes with music, effects, or environmental noise, check intelligibility, separate
speech/music tracks, noise cancellation, and speech-clarity profiles. Hearing-device frequency
shaping and noise processing vary; competing sound can remain a barrier, so device use does not
establish that separation is unnecessary.

**AT Mechanisms**: Hearing device processing, audio settings **Device context**: All

**UI Requirements**: Speech should be clearly separated from background audio. Ideal: separate
volume controls for speech and background audio. Video players that offer a "dialogue boost" or
"speech enhancement" mode are valuable. Audio mixing that buries speech under music or sound effects
is a significant barrier. For calls and meetings, noise suppression features that reduce background
noise on both the sending and receiving ends improve clarity.

### Visual scanning and layout comprehension _(from shared)_

When visual scanning is available and preferred, page regions such as the header, navigation, main
content, sidebar, and footer can help establish layout. Visible control affordances and hierarchy
through headings, spacing, colour, and size may help locate actions. Do not assume that visual
scanning is available or that these cues work alone.

**AT Mechanisms**: Visual perception **Device context**: All

**UI Requirements**: Clear visual hierarchy through headings, spacing, and grouping. Interactive
elements must look interactive (visible borders, underlines, or other affordances). Primary actions
should be visually prominent.

### Mouse / pointer interaction _(from shared)_

Mouse, trackpad, or touch-pointer interaction may involve clicking, selecting text, dragging,
hovering for tooltips, or opening context menus. Evaluate the actions supported by the chosen input
method; hover and sustained dragging are not available in every pointer context.

**AT Mechanisms**: Mouse, Trackpad, Touchscreen **Device context**: Desktop

**UI Requirements**: Interactive elements are easy to activate with the intended pointer action and
are far enough apart that users do not accidentally activate adjacent controls. Hover states should
indicate interactivity. Drag-and-drop should have clear affordances.

### Reading and scrolling _(from shared)_

Visual reading may proceed sequentially or through heading scans, with scrolling to reach content
beyond the viewport. Evaluate logical flow appropriate to the writing system, such as top-to-bottom
and left-to-right in LTR content. Do not assume one reading direction or scrolling method across
languages and input configurations.

**AT Mechanisms**: Visual reading, Mouse wheel / scroll gesture **Device context**: All

**UI Requirements**: Text must be readable: sufficient contrast, reasonable font size, adequate line
spacing. Content should not require horizontal scrolling at standard viewport widths. Long pages
should provide navigation aids (table of contents, back-to-top).

### Form interaction _(from shared)_

Form tasks may involve reaching fields, typing, selecting options, checking boxes, and submitting
through different input methods. Evaluate clear labels, placeholder text that does not replace
labels, and actionable error feedback without assuming pointer access.

**AT Mechanisms**: Keyboard (typing), Mouse (clicking fields), Visual reading (labels) **Device
context**: All

**UI Requirements**: Every form field must have a visible label. Required fields must be clearly
indicated. Error messages must be specific, adjacent to the field, and visible without scrolling.
Successful submission must be clearly confirmed.

### Navigation and wayfinding _(from shared)_

Menus, breadcrumbs, links, and browser back/forward can support navigation between pages and views.
Evaluate whether the current location, available destinations, and return paths are understandable
through the access strategies in scope.

**AT Mechanisms**: Visual scanning, Mouse clicking, Browser controls **Device context**: All

**UI Requirements**: Consistent navigation across pages. Current location clearly indicated (active
nav item, breadcrumbs). Important actions reachable from any page. Browser back button should work
predictably.

### Touch / pointer interaction _(from shared)_

Touch interaction may include taps, swipes, pinch-zoom, or long presses, depending on the device and
access method. Direct touch has no hover preview; evaluate discoverable controls, target size, and
alternatives to complex gestures. Do not require every touch interaction pattern to be available to
the same person.

**AT Mechanisms**: Touchscreen tap, Swipe gestures, Pinch-zoom **Device context**: Mobile

**UI Requirements**: Touch targets are easy to activate without accidentally activating adjacent
controls — small or tightly packed targets force users to retry or hit the wrong control. No
reliance on hover for essential information. Custom gestures must have single-pointer alternatives.
Drag-and-drop must have tap-based alternatives. No accidental activation from scroll gestures.

## Interaction Contexts

### User with assistive hearing device

When hearing aids or a cochlear implant are used, check direct Bluetooth streaming, noise
cancellation, clear encoding, and adjustable volume. Quiet speech may be intelligible, while noise,
overlapping speakers, unfamiliar accents, compression artifacts, echo, or low bitrate can reduce
clarity; outcomes vary. Captions may supplement or replace listening in difficult conditions.
High-frequency alerts may remain unavailable outside a device's correction range.

**Primary AT**: Hearing aids and cochlear implants, Caption systems (supplementary) **Device
context**: All **Preferred navigation**: Visual scanning and layout comprehension, Mouse / pointer
interaction, Checking audio quality and volume controls

**Expectations**:

- Audio output is clean and well-encoded (no heavy compression, echo, or low bitrate)
- Volume controls are available and go loud enough
- Background noise can be reduced or separated from speech
- Captions available as an optional supplement for difficult listening conditions
- Audio alerts are at perceivable frequencies and volumes, with visual backups
- Voice/video calls offer noise cancellation and audio quality settings
- Mono audio option is available for users with asymmetric hearing loss

### User without assistive hearing device

Without a hearing aid or cochlear implant in the interaction, check system volume, app boost,
equaliser, mono audio, captions, and visual redundancy without inferring why a device is absent.
Choice, suitability, or fitting access may be relevant but are not evidence about an individual.
High volume may affect others or expose private content in shared spaces; assess alternatives
without ranking needs against device users.

**Primary AT**: Enhanced audio settings, Caption systems (supplementary), Mono audio output **Device
context**: All **Preferred navigation**: Visual scanning and layout comprehension, Mouse / pointer
interaction, Looking for captions as supplement

**Expectations**:

- Volume controls are prominent and offer significant boost beyond default
- Captions available and toggleable for all video/audio content
- Visual indicators for all sounds and audio alerts
- Mono audio option available system-wide and in the application
- Audio does not auto-play at unexpected volumes
- Quiet or subtle audio cues always have a visual backup
- Speech in video/audio content is not buried under music or sound effects

### New / infrequent user _(from shared)_

First-time or infrequent interaction may require rediscovering controls and workflows. Labels,
accessible affordances, and progressive disclosure can support learning without requiring prior
knowledge of shortcuts or hidden features.

**Primary AT**: None **Device context**: All **Preferred navigation**: Visual scanning and layout
comprehension, Navigation and wayfinding, Form interaction

**Expectations**:

- Interface is learnable without documentation
- Primary actions are visually obvious
- Error messages guide toward resolution
- Navigation structure is understandable

### Expert / frequent user _(from shared)_

Frequent interaction may build familiarity with layouts, shortcuts, and workflows. Consistency and
efficient paths can support established strategies; changes may require relearning. Expertise in a
task does not establish expertise with every assistive technology.

**Primary AT**: None **Device context**: All **Preferred navigation**: Mouse / pointer interaction,
Navigation and wayfinding

**Expectations**:

- Keyboard shortcuts for common tasks
- Consistent layout across sessions and pages
- Efficient paths to frequently used features
- Changes to familiar workflows are clearly communicated

## Learning Considerations

- **Discovering audio enhancement settings**: The user needs to find volume controls, equaliser
  settings, mono audio toggles, and noise cancellation options. If these are buried in system
  accessibility settings rather than surfaced in the application's audio controls, the user may not
  discover them. Applications that provide their own audio enhancement options (volume boost, noise
  reduction) should make them discoverable from the audio/video player or call interface.

- **Caption toggle discovery**: When conditions change mid-video or mid-call, captions may need to
  be enabled quickly. Pausing, opening a menu, and visiting a separate settings page can lose
  additional content. Keep controls immediately accessible during playback without assuming who
  always needs captions.

- **Audio quality calibration**: Check first-use adjustment of device settings or volume. Variation
  between calls, during screen sharing, or under reduced bandwidth can require repeated
  recalibration. Consistent high-quality audio reduces that adjustment effort.

- **Learning which sounds have visual backups**: Inconsistent audio/visual pairing can make feedback
  coverage unpredictable and increase concern about missed cues. Check consistent pairing rather
  than attributing anxiety or stress to everyone in a group.

- **Volume management in shared spaces**: When the user compensates for hearing loss by turning
  volume high, they may inadvertently expose private content to others nearby. They learn which
  environments are safe for loud audio and when to switch to captions-only or text alternatives. The
  interface's volume and caption controls affect this privacy trade-off.

- **First visit** _(from shared)_: Familiar web, desktop, and mobile conventions may support
  learning, but recognition of hamburger menus, search icons, tabs, or modals is not guaranteed.
  Explain unfamiliar patterns and expose their purpose through accessible labels and feedback.

- **Mental models** _(from shared)_: Early interactions may establish expectations. Inconsistent
  behavior, such as a button that sometimes navigates and sometimes submits, can undermine
  confidence and require additional checking.

- **Error recovery** _(from shared)_: Support recovery from mistakes through undo, appropriate
  confirmation dialogs, and clear error messages. Make the consequences of irreversible actions
  clear rather than assuming every mistake can be avoided.

- **Cognitive load** _(from shared)_: Clutter, excessive options, and unclear hierarchy can increase
  attention and memory demands. Evaluate those demands in the task context without inferring a
  person's cognitive capabilities.

## Heuristic Lens Overrides

### Heuristic 1 — Visibility of System Status

Chimes, completion tones, and error sounds may be partly heard, intermittent, or missed depending on
conditions. Check visual redundancy for every audio status cue in quiet and noisy settings; do not
presume quiet guarantees perception. Visual status must remain available independently of current
listening conditions.

### Heuristic 2 — Match Between System and Real World

Check clear, well-paced speech without competing noise, compression artifacts, or music. These
factors may impair intelligibility even when sound is audible or a hearing device is used. Assess
production quality and separate speech/non-speech tracks where possible, without assigning a fixed
processing capability.

### Heuristic 3 — User Control and Freedom

Check controls for volume, captions, stereo/mono, noise cancellation, playback speed, pause, and
replay during playback. Slower speech may aid comprehension but is not a universal preference. Call
input/output adjustments should not disrupt the call.

### Heuristic 4 — Consistency and Standards

Check consistent caption coverage, visual indicators, and encoding quality across content.
Sound-only exceptions can undermine a chosen access strategy; inconsistent quality can require
device or volume recalibration for each item.

### Heuristic 5 — Error Prevention

Audio-only error indicators that the user may or may not hear are unreliable error prevention. An
error tone the user misses in a noisy environment means the error goes unnoticed until later
consequences. Visual error indicators must always accompany audio ones. For forms, inline validation
that provides both visual and (optional) audio feedback is ideal — the visual component is the
reliable channel, and the audio is a supplement.

### Heuristic 6 — Recognition Rather than Recall

Audio enhancement controls (volume boost, captions, mono audio, noise cancellation) must be
discoverable in context. The user should not have to recall that caption settings are in
Accessibility > Audio > Captioning > Settings when they need to toggle captions during a video call.
Frequently used audio controls should be surfaced in the media player or call interface, not buried
in system preferences.

### Heuristic 7 — Flexibility and Efficiency of Use

The interface should support the user's variable hearing ability across conditions. In quiet
conditions with a good audio connection, the user may not need captions; in noisy conditions or with
poor audio quality, captions become essential. Quick-toggle controls for captions and volume
adjustment support this flexibility. The user should not have to navigate away from the current task
to adjust audio settings.

### Heuristic 8 — Aesthetic and Minimalist Design

For this consideration, audio clutter — background music that competes with speech, ambient sounds
that add atmosphere but reduce speech clarity, multiple simultaneous audio streams — is a functional
barrier, not an aesthetic choice. Audio design should prioritise speech clarity. Optional audio
elements (background music, sound effects) should be separately controllable or disableable.
Captions, when enabled, should be styled to be readable without being visually overwhelming.

### Heuristic 9 — Help Users Recognize, Diagnose, and Recover from Errors

When the user misses an audio error cue, they may not know something went wrong until they encounter
the downstream consequence. Visual error indicators with clear, specific messages are essential. For
calls: if audio quality degrades (the user hears garbled speech), the system should indicate the
quality issue visually — the user may not be sure whether they're experiencing hearing difficulty or
a network problem. "Your connection quality is poor" helps them diagnose the source.

### Heuristic 10 — Help and Documentation

Documentation should be available in text form, not only as video or audio tutorials. If video
tutorials exist, they must have captions and clear audio. Help content explaining audio enhancement
features (how to enable noise cancellation, adjust equaliser, toggle mono audio, customise captions)
should be easy to find. For hearing device users, documentation about Bluetooth audio streaming
compatibility or optimised audio settings is valuable.
