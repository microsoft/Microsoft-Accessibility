# FPS 4.2.10 — Usage with limited cognition, language or learning (numeracy and mathematical concepts)

The ICT provides features and/or presentation that makes it simpler and easier to understand,
operate, and use.

## Evaluation Considerations

Evaluate possible barriers involving numbers, quantities, magnitude, and mathematical concepts,
including varied requirements discussed in dyscalculia research. No one pattern represents everyone.
Do not simulate a person, infer intelligence, or declare sensory, motor, language, or other
cognitive capabilities unaffected; requirements may overlap across lenses.

Assess reading and comparing numbers, relative magnitude (greater, cheaper, sooner), and differences
among 10, 100, and 1000. Quantity estimation, arithmetic, unit conversion, and percentages may need
support. Numeric-only schedules, "15:34 UTC", and ambiguous dates may be confusing. Prices, totals,
and choice costs should not require unsupported addition; errors may remain hard to detect even
after repeated checking. These are possible task barriers, not universal abilities or behaviors.

For each screen or flow, consider:

- **NUMBERS**: Is essential information conveyed only as raw numbers, or also as words and concepts
  (cold / warm / hot; small / medium / large)?
- **MAGNITUDE**: Must the user compare or judge relative size, distance, time, or price without
  support — for example, to tell which option is cheaper or sooner?
- **QUANTITY**: Does ordering or selecting require judging quantity (six bananas versus one bunch of
  six)? Are unusual or extreme quantities flagged before they are committed?
- **UNITS**: Are metrics given in units the user is likely to know, defaulted to their locale, with
  familiar alternatives — or must the user convert?
- **CALCULATION**: Does the task require mental arithmetic (a total, a multiplication, working out
  the next departure)? Could the system compute it instead?
- **NUMERIC TIME**: Are dates, times, durations, and schedules given only as figures or relative
  numbers, or are concrete cues provided (a "now" marker on a timeline, a written-out date, "your
  train leaves in 10 minutes")?
- **MONEY**: Are charges, totals, and the cost of each choice stated clearly and early, rather than
  left for the user to add up or discover at the end?
- **ALTERNATIVES**: Do charts and graphs come with plain summaries and non-numeric explanations? Are
  key numbers reinforced with words, color, or pictures?

Possible access barriers include: temperature, size, or distance shown only as a number; relative
magnitude with no concrete reference; quantity judgments while ordering; unit mismatches that force
a conversion; totals and other arithmetic the user must do in their head; schedules and dates given
only numerically or in an ambiguous format; surprise totals at the end of a purchase; charts and
graphs with no summary; and PINs, codes, or card-verification numbers that must be recalled and
typed.

IMPORTANT — Audience-appropriate language and terminology:

Calibrate to the audience and domain. A product whose users are quantitative professionals — a
trading console, an engineering calculator, a statistics package — legitimately presents numbers and
mathematics as core content, and its users need that content. Do not flag necessary numeric or
mathematical content as a defect for an audience that requires it, any more than you would flag
domain vocabulary for an expert user base.

For this consideration the concern is different from the mere presence of numbers: it is whether the
interface forces the **user** to perform calculations, judge magnitude, or decode raw figures when
the system could instead compute the result, offer a non-numeric alternative, or supply a concrete
reference. Flag required mental arithmetic, relative-magnitude judgments with no support, unit
mismatches and forced conversions, ambiguous numeric formats, and numbers presented with no
reinforcing word, concept, or visual. Do not flag numeric or mathematical content that the intended
audience expects and needs — flag the absence of support around it.

## Assistive Technologies

Evaluate calculation, conversion, normalization, non-numeric cues, and mainstream locale/formatting
features. Tool use varies and can overlap with language, memory, sensory, or motor supports; this
numeric focus does not bound a person's overall requirements.

### Calculator and conversion tools

**Category**: calculation-aid **Platforms**: OS, web, mobile, browser extensions **Device context**:
All

Calculators and unit/currency converters offload arithmetic, comparison, and conversion where mental
calculation is difficult or undesired. Test selectable values rather than numbers baked into images,
and system-computed results that avoid transcription.

### Text-to-speech for numbers

**Category**: read-aloud **Platforms**: Windows, macOS, iOS, Android, extensions **Device context**:
All

Read-aloud tools can speak numbers so the user hears rather than visually decodes them, which helps
some users confirm a figure. The support is fragile: long numbers, phone numbers, codes, Roman
numerals, and unusual symbols are frequently misread or chunked wrongly by text-to-speech. It
depends on numbers being correctly formatted and marked up, with locale-appropriate separators and
context, so the spoken output matches the intended value.

### Spreadsheet and visual aids

**Category**: visual-aid **Platforms**: Spreadsheet apps, OS, extensions **Device context**: Desktop

Color coding, increased spacing, and larger fonts can help isolate relevant figures and retain place
in shared spreadsheets and dense numeric tables. Evaluate the ability to apply visual distinctions
without color alone carrying meaning. Undifferentiated numeric grids may increase effort, anxiety,
or errors; responses vary.

### Personalization semantics for non-numeric concepts

**Category**: personalization **Platforms**: Browser, extensions **Device context**: All

Personalization mechanisms can substitute or augment numbers with concepts such as "cold / warm /
hot" or "small / medium / large" through exposed semantics. Users or chosen helpers may set
preferences for words over figures. Evaluate meaningful annotations while preserving exact figures
where task-essential.

### Platform locale and unit settings

**Category**: platform-feature **Platforms**: All **Device context**: All

Platform locale handling can supply familiar numbers, currency, units, and dates without conversion.
Ignoring it may impose unfamiliar temperature units, date order, or currency conversion. Evaluate
defaults and preferred alternatives rather than equate locale with individual familiarity.

## Exploration Strategies

### Concept-over-number reading

Evaluate concept-supported reading: "hot" with a temperature, "large" with a measurement, or "almost
full" with a percentage. Raw figures may require effortful or error-prone interpretation; concepts
alongside them can support decisions. Retain exact figures where essential.

**AT Mechanisms**: Visual reading, Personalization (non-numeric concepts) **Device context**: All

**UI Requirements**: Reinforce important numbers with non-numeric concepts (words, categories,
descriptions). Where understanding the exact figure is not essential to the task, provide a verbal
or visual equivalent. Do not rely on the user to infer meaning from a bare number.

### Offloading calculation

Test tool- or system-computed totals, conversions, and next-step results rather than required mental
arithmetic. Unsupported calculation may block completion in some contexts. Evaluate inline
computation or easy transfer of tool results instead of assigning a universal calculation ability.

**AT Mechanisms**: Calculator / conversion tools, Form autofill **Device context**: All

**UI Requirements**: Perform calculations for the user — totals, subtotals, conversions, durations —
rather than requiring mental arithmetic. Show computed results explicitly. Where a value must be
entered, accept it in any reasonable format and avoid making the user compute it first.

### Magnitude-by-proxy judgment

Evaluate product pictures, "you are half way" messages, color scales with non-color meaning, "now"
markers, and "cheaper / dearer" labels for relative size, distance, time, or price. Comparing 0.9 m
with 900 mm or deciding which option is sooner may require support. Concrete references can reduce
unsupported quantitative judgments.

**AT Mechanisms**: Visual reading, Visual aids **Device context**: All

**UI Requirements**: Provide concrete references for magnitude — images of real size, progress and
"now" indicators, relative labels (cheaper, sooner, larger), and visual scales. Do not require the
user to compare or rank raw numbers to make a decision.

### Verification and double-checking

Evaluate phone numbers, codes, quantities, and totals where errors may not be obvious even after
repeated checking. Pre-commit summaries and unusual-value warnings (large quantities or unexpected
totals) can provide support without assuming a person's error-detection ability.

**AT Mechanisms**: Visual reading, Read-aloud (number confirmation) **Device context**: All

**UI Requirements**: Show a clear summary of numeric choices (quantities, dates, totals) before
final commitment, with the chance to correct them without losing other data. Warn about unusual or
extreme values. Divide long numbers into chunks to make checking easier. Do not depend on the user
noticing that a number is wrong.

### Format-and-unit normalization

Test familiar units, currency, distances, temperatures, and date order through locale defaults and
selectable alternatives. Unfamiliar representations may require conversion or cause confusion.
Locale does not prove individual familiarity or preference.

**AT Mechanisms**: Platform locale settings, Calculator / conversion tools **Device context**: All

**UI Requirements**: Default numbers, units, currency, and date formats to the user's locale, and
offer familiar alternatives where a fixed unit is unavoidable. Write dates unambiguously (spell out
the month). Keep number formatting consistent and locale-appropriate so values are read as intended.

### Visual scanning and layout comprehension _(from shared)_

When visual scanning is available and preferred, page regions such as the header, navigation, main
content, sidebar, and footer can help establish layout. Visible control affordances and hierarchy
through headings, spacing, colour, and size may help locate actions. Do not assume that visual
scanning is available or that these cues work alone.

**AT Mechanisms**: Visual perception **Device context**: All

**UI Requirements**: Clear visual hierarchy through headings, spacing, and grouping. Interactive
elements must look interactive (visible borders, underlines, or other affordances). Primary actions
should be visually prominent.

### Mouse / pointer interaction _(from shared)_

Mouse, trackpad, or touch-pointer interaction may involve clicking, selecting text, dragging,
hovering for tooltips, or opening context menus. Evaluate the actions supported by the chosen input
method; hover and sustained dragging are not available in every pointer context.

**AT Mechanisms**: Mouse, Trackpad, Touchscreen **Device context**: Desktop

**UI Requirements**: Interactive elements are easy to activate with the intended pointer action and
are far enough apart that users do not accidentally activate adjacent controls. Hover states should
indicate interactivity. Drag-and-drop should have clear affordances.

### Reading and scrolling _(from shared)_

Visual reading may proceed sequentially or through heading scans, with scrolling to reach content
beyond the viewport. Evaluate logical flow appropriate to the writing system, such as top-to-bottom
and left-to-right in LTR content. Do not assume one reading direction or scrolling method across
languages and input configurations.

**AT Mechanisms**: Visual reading, Mouse wheel / scroll gesture **Device context**: All

**UI Requirements**: Text must be readable: sufficient contrast, reasonable font size, adequate line
spacing. Content should not require horizontal scrolling at standard viewport widths. Long pages
should provide navigation aids (table of contents, back-to-top).

### Form interaction _(from shared)_

Form tasks may involve reaching fields, typing, selecting options, checking boxes, and submitting
through different input methods. Evaluate clear labels, placeholder text that does not replace
labels, and actionable error feedback without assuming pointer access.

**AT Mechanisms**: Keyboard (typing), Mouse (clicking fields), Visual reading (labels) **Device
context**: All

**UI Requirements**: Every form field must have a visible label. Required fields must be clearly
indicated. Error messages must be specific, adjacent to the field, and visible without scrolling.
Successful submission must be clearly confirmed.

### Navigation and wayfinding _(from shared)_

Menus, breadcrumbs, links, and browser back/forward can support navigation between pages and views.
Evaluate whether the current location, available destinations, and return paths are understandable
through the access strategies in scope.

**AT Mechanisms**: Visual scanning, Mouse clicking, Browser controls **Device context**: All

**UI Requirements**: Consistent navigation across pages. Current location clearly indicated (active
nav item, breadcrumbs). Important actions reachable from any page. Browser back button should work
predictably.

### Touch / pointer interaction _(from shared)_

Touch interaction may include taps, swipes, pinch-zoom, or long presses, depending on the device and
access method. Direct touch has no hover preview; evaluate discoverable controls, target size, and
alternatives to complex gestures. Do not require every touch interaction pattern to be available to
the same person.

**AT Mechanisms**: Touchscreen tap, Swipe gestures, Pinch-zoom **Device context**: Mobile

**UI Requirements**: Touch targets are easy to activate without accidentally activating adjacent
controls — small or tightly packed targets force users to retry or hit the wrong control. No
reliance on hover for essential information. Custom gestures must have single-pointer alternatives.
Drag-and-drop must have tap-based alternatives. No accidental activation from scroll gestures.

## Interaction Contexts

### Number reading and calculation-support contexts

Evaluate reading, comparing, and calculating where supports are needed, including counting on
fingers for basic sums. This does not establish intelligence or capability in other areas. Check
system-computed results, words or visuals explaining figures, and paths without required mental
arithmetic; interpreting a bare figure may itself need support.

**Primary AT**: Calculator / conversion tools, Personalization **Device context**: All **Preferred
navigation**: Offloading calculation, Concept-over-number reading, Verification and double-checking

**Expectations**:

- Totals, subtotals, and conversions are computed by the system, not the user
- Important numbers are reinforced with words, concepts, or visuals
- No task requires mental arithmetic to complete
- Numbers can be entered in any reasonable format and are checked back to the user

### Quantity and magnitude-support contexts

Evaluate quantity, size, distance, and cost judgments where unsupported comparisons may cause over-
or under-ordering or obscure a better deal. Check actual-product and size pictures, relative labels,
and unusual-quantity or total warnings. Requirements vary by task and familiarity.

**Primary AT**: Visual aids, Calculator / conversion tools **Device context**: All **Preferred
navigation**: Magnitude-by-proxy judgment, Verification and double-checking, Concept-over-number
reading

**Expectations**:

- Concrete references for size and quantity (images, examples, relative terms)
- Warnings when a quantity or total is unusually large or small, before it is committed
- Clear "cheaper / sooner / larger" comparisons rather than raw numbers to rank
- A summary of quantities and costs before final commitment, easily corrected

### Numeric time, date, and sequence-support contexts

Evaluate next-train calculations, whether a meeting time has passed, sequencing time-bound steps,
and ambiguous dates. Concrete temporal cues and system-handled date/time logic may help where raw
times, dates, or durations are difficult to interpret.

**Primary AT**: Platform locale settings, Calculator / conversion tools **Device context**: All
**Preferred navigation**: Magnitude-by-proxy judgment, Format-and-unit normalization, Verification
and double-checking

**Expectations**:

- Concrete time cues (a "now" marker, "in 10 minutes", written-out dates) rather than bare figures
- Dates written unambiguously and defaulted to the user's locale and conventions
- The system prevents invalid date/time choices (such as a return before a departure)
- Durations and "how long until" are computed and shown, not left to the user

### New / infrequent user _(from shared)_

First-time or infrequent interaction may require rediscovering controls and workflows. Labels,
accessible affordances, and progressive disclosure can support learning without requiring prior
knowledge of shortcuts or hidden features.

**Primary AT**: None **Device context**: All **Preferred navigation**: Visual scanning and layout
comprehension, Navigation and wayfinding, Form interaction

**Expectations**:

- Interface is learnable without documentation
- Primary actions are visually obvious
- Error messages guide toward resolution
- Navigation structure is understandable

### Expert / frequent user _(from shared)_

Frequent interaction may build familiarity with layouts, shortcuts, and workflows. Consistency and
efficient paths can support established strategies; changes may require relearning. Expertise in a
task does not establish expertise with every assistive technology.

**Primary AT**: None **Device context**: All **Preferred navigation**: Mouse / pointer interaction,
Navigation and wayfinding

**Expectations**:

- Keyboard shortcuts for common tasks
- Consistent layout across sessions and pages
- Efficient paths to frequently used features
- Changes to familiar workflows are clearly communicated

## Learning Considerations

- **Numeracy difficulty does not establish overall capability**: Do not infer intelligence or other
  abilities from numeric difficulty, or assume no overlapping requirements. Target numeric support
  to numbers, magnitude, units, and calculation rather than indiscriminately simplify the interface.

- **Numeric errors may need checking support**: A wrong code, quantity, or total may remain
  unnoticed even after checking. Evaluate pre-commit summaries and unusual-value warnings without
  claiming text errors would be easier or defining an internal error-detection ability.

- **Magnitude may need an anchor**: Pictures, comparisons, and progress markers can make size,
  distance, time, or price meaningful where bare figures are hard to interpret. Evaluate concrete
  references alongside correct numbers.

- **Conversions may compound difficulty**: Unfamiliar units can add a conversion barrier even when a
  value is readable. Evaluate locale defaults and familiar alternatives rather than presume
  individual conversion capability.

- **Calculation can be a barrier**: Summing a cart, finding a duration, or comparing prices may
  block completion where arithmetic is unsupported. Evaluate system-computed results even when
  calculation appears simple.

- **First visit** _(from shared)_: Familiar web, desktop, and mobile conventions may support
  learning, but recognition of hamburger menus, search icons, tabs, or modals is not guaranteed.
  Explain unfamiliar patterns and expose their purpose through accessible labels and feedback.

- **Mental models** _(from shared)_: Early interactions may establish expectations. Inconsistent
  behavior, such as a button that sometimes navigates and sometimes submits, can undermine
  confidence and require additional checking.

- **Error recovery** _(from shared)_: Support recovery from mistakes through undo, appropriate
  confirmation dialogs, and clear error messages. Make the consequences of irreversible actions
  clear rather than assuming every mistake can be avoided.

- **Cognitive load** _(from shared)_: Clutter, excessive options, and unclear hierarchy can increase
  attention and memory demands. Evaluate those demands in the task context without inferring a
  person's cognitive capabilities.

## Heuristic Lens Overrides

### Heuristic 1 — Visibility of System Status

Evaluate concrete status alongside quantity, progress, or time: "about 2 minutes left" with a
progress bar, "almost done" with a percentage, or "now" on a timeline. Bare percentages, counts, or
timestamps may be difficult to interpret. Words and visuals can support meaning without replacing
task-essential figures.

### Heuristic 2 — Match Between System and Real World

Evaluate real-world concepts alongside figures: "cold / warm / hot", "small / medium / large", and
"in 10 minutes", with familiar locale units. Apply the audience principle from Evaluation
Considerations: necessary numeric and mathematical content for quantitative audiences is not a
defect. Check concrete references, units, and calculation support, not the mere presence of numbers.

### Heuristic 3 — User Control and Freedom

Evaluate changes to quantities, units, and numeric inputs and reversal of number-driven actions.
Warn before costly large quantities, expensive choices, or non-refundable dates. Where wrong values
are difficult to detect, provide an easy correction path when discovered.

### Heuristic 4 — Consistency and Standards

Keep units, number formats, and date conventions consistent across the entire interface; switching
between metric and imperial, between currencies, or between date orders mid-flow forces conversions
and breeds error. Present the same kind of value the same way everywhere so the user can rely on a
single, stable interpretation rather than re-checking what each number means in each place.

### Heuristic 5 — Error Prevention

Prevent numeric errors structurally: compute totals and durations, validate and warn on unusual
quantities, accept reasonable formats, chunk long numbers, use sensible units and dates, and prevent
impossible choices (return before departure). Avoid required recall of PINs, codes, or
card-verification numbers; later detection may require additional support.

### Heuristic 6 — Recognition Rather than Recall

Show the meaning of a number so the user can recognize it rather than compute or recall it: surface
the running total, the converted value, the relative comparison, and the consequence of a choice
directly in the interface. Do not expect the user to hold figures in mind, add them up, or remember
a quantity from an earlier step — present the result they need to see, when they need to see it.

### Heuristic 7 — Flexibility and Efficiency of Use

Evaluate non-numeric alternatives and persistent personalization for words, concepts, familiar
units, and visuals. Provide inline calculators, converters, and computed results plus saved lists
and reusable quantities from successful orders. Efficiency means reducing unsupported quantitative
work, not only adding numeric shortcuts.

### Heuristic 8 — Aesthetic and Minimalist Design

Evaluate findability of relevant values in dense grids. Use color alongside spacing and larger type,
reduce competing figures, and de-emphasize secondary values without losing necessary information.
Undifferentiated numeric grids may increase anxiety or errors; effects vary.

### Heuristic 9 — Help Users Recognize, Diagnose, and Recover from Errors

Numeric error messages must explain the problem in words and show the expected value or format with
a concrete example — not "invalid amount" but "Enter a whole number of tickets, for example 2".
Preserve the user's other entries through the correction, and where a value is merely unusual rather
than invalid, warn and confirm ("That is 60 items — is that right?") before it causes harm, since
the user may not realize the figure is off.

### Heuristic 10 — Help and Documentation

Evaluate worked examples, non-numeric explanations, visuals, and plain chart/graph summaries.
Provide an easy human-support route where quantitative steps need additional help. Restating a
number or formula alone may leave the same barrier in place; concepts, examples, and pictures should
explain meaning while preserving accuracy.
