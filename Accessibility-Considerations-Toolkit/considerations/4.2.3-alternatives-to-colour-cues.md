# FPS 4.2.3 — Usage without perception of colour

Where ICT provides visual modes of operation, the ICT provides a visual mode of operation that does
not require user perception of colour.

## Evaluation Considerations

Evaluate whether visual information remains distinguishable without relying on colour perception.
Check text, shapes, layout, icons, and images for non-colour cues; do not impersonate a person with
a colour vision deficiency or assume those elements are otherwise perceivable.

The primary question is whether ANY information is conveyed through colour alone. If colour is the
only visual difference between states, categories, or meanings, the distinction fails this
evaluation condition.

Look for:

- Status indicators that rely only on colour (red/green dots, coloured badges)
- Error/success/warning feedback communicated only by text or border colour
- Links within text distinguished only by colour (no underline or other treatment)
- Charts, graphs, or data visualisations using colour-coded legends
- Form validation that uses only colour to indicate valid/invalid fields
- Colour-coded categories, tags, or labels without text or icon alternatives
- Required field indicators that are colour-only (e.g., red asterisk with no other indication)

IMPORTANT — What counts as 'colour alone':

Perception of UI elements depends on a COMBINATION of visual attributes, not any single attribute in
isolation. Research on visual perception of controls (e.g., Material Design's non-text contrast
study) shows that identifiability comes from multiple inner attributes of a component (font weight,
fill, stroke, elevation, size, iconography, semantic familiarity) and outer attributes of its
context (spacing, layout, position, alignment, adjacent elements, expectation given context). It is
the combination of these attributes that aids identification.

A 'colour alone' violation occurs ONLY when colour is the SOLE attribute distinguishing two states,
categories, or meanings — when removing the colour difference would leave no other visual attribute
to tell them apart. If other attributes in combination provide sufficient differentiation (e.g.,
fill vs. no fill, shape change, border weight, iconography, text, position, size, typographic
treatment, interactive behaviours like keyboard-navigable callouts on chart data points), colour is
NOT the sole means for that information under 1.4.1. Verify the alternative's meaning and
availability rather than treating its mere presence as proof of overall conformance.

When assessing whether colour is used alone, consider:

- What inner attributes of the component change between states? (fill, stroke, shape, icon, text,
  size, elevation)
- What outer/contextual attributes reinforce the distinction? (position, spacing, adjacent labels,
  layout expectations)
- If you removed the colour difference entirely, could you still tell the states apart from the
  remaining attributes?

If yes — the remaining attributes provide sufficient differentiation — do NOT flag a colour-alone
violation.

Keep findings under this lens focused on colour-dependent information. Text readability, layout,
keyboard operation, focus, audio, timing, and programmatic semantics require their own checks; they
are not presumed accessible or irrelevant to a person's needs. Where an interactive non-colour
alternative is claimed, verify its operability before relying on it.

## Assistive Technologies

### Windows Colour Filters

**Category**: colour-filter **Platforms**: Windows **Device context**: Desktop

Windows includes built-in colour filters (Settings > Accessibility > Colour filters) that remap the
display to simulate or compensate for colour vision deficiencies. Modes include Greyscale, Inverted,
Greyscale inverted, Red-green (protanopia/deuteranopia), and Blue-yellow (tritanopia). The user may
or may not have these enabled. The filter affects the entire display — it does not change the
underlying content, so colour-only information remains colour-only even with a filter active.

### macOS Colour Filters

**Category**: colour-filter **Platforms**: macOS **Device context**: Desktop

macOS provides colour filters (System Settings > Accessibility > Display > Colour Filters) with
options for Greyscale, Red/Green filter (protanopia/deuteranopia), and Blue/Yellow filter
(tritanopia), plus a colour tint option. Like Windows, these remap display output but cannot add
non-colour information that the content does not already provide.

## Exploration Strategies

### Scanning for non-colour cues

Check text labels, icons, patterns, shapes, position, borders, and underlines for meaning
independent of colour. Red vs. green status dots without another cue cannot distinguish states under
this lens.

**AT Mechanisms**: Visual scanning (reduced colour discrimination) **Device context**: All

**UI Requirements**: Every piece of information conveyed through colour must also be conveyed
through at least one additional visual channel: text, iconography, shape, pattern, border style,
position, or size. Colour can reinforce meaning, but it must not be the sole means of conveying
information.

### Seeking explicit labels and legends

For colour-coded charts, maps, status, and validation, check labels, legends, or tooltips that
restate meaning. A colour-only legend can prevent matching the data series; direct labels and
matching non-colour markers provide alternatives.

**AT Mechanisms**: Visual scanning, Hover/focus for tooltips **Device context**: All

**UI Requirements**: Charts and graphs should use direct labels, patterns, or textures in addition
to colour. Legends should use patterns or shapes that match the data series, not colour swatches
alone. Status indicators should include text or icons alongside colour.

### Identifying interactive elements by shape and affordance

Check whether button borders and link underlines or other affordances establish interactivity
without colour differences. Blue text alone can leave a link indistinguishable from body text when
hue is not available as a cue.

**AT Mechanisms**: Visual scanning **Device context**: All

**UI Requirements**: Links within body text must be distinguishable by more than colour alone — use
underline, font weight, border, or other visual treatment. Buttons must have visible borders or fill
that distinguish them from surrounding text regardless of colour.

### Interpreting state and feedback without colour

Check error, success, warning, active-tab, and selected-item states beyond red, green, or blue
shifts. Icons (✓, ✗, ⚠), labels ('Error:', 'Success'), border changes, and position shifts must
carry distinguishable meaning rather than merely accompany colour.

**AT Mechanisms**: Visual scanning **Device context**: All

**UI Requirements**: Error states must include an icon or text label, not just a red border or red
text. Success feedback must include text or an icon, not just green. Active/selected states must use
a non-colour indicator: bold weight, underline, border, position, or icon.

### Visual scanning and layout comprehension _(from shared)_

When visual scanning is available and preferred, page regions such as the header, navigation, main
content, sidebar, and footer can help establish layout. Visible control affordances and hierarchy
through headings, spacing, colour, and size may help locate actions. Do not assume that visual
scanning is available or that these cues work alone.

**AT Mechanisms**: Visual perception **Device context**: All

**UI Requirements**: Clear visual hierarchy through headings, spacing, and grouping. Interactive
elements must look interactive (visible borders, underlines, or other affordances). Primary actions
should be visually prominent.

### Mouse / pointer interaction _(from shared)_

Mouse, trackpad, or touch-pointer interaction may involve clicking, selecting text, dragging,
hovering for tooltips, or opening context menus. Evaluate the actions supported by the chosen input
method; hover and sustained dragging are not available in every pointer context.

**AT Mechanisms**: Mouse, Trackpad, Touchscreen **Device context**: Desktop

**UI Requirements**: Interactive elements are easy to activate with the intended pointer action and
are far enough apart that users do not accidentally activate adjacent controls. Hover states should
indicate interactivity. Drag-and-drop should have clear affordances.

### Reading and scrolling _(from shared)_

Visual reading may proceed sequentially or through heading scans, with scrolling to reach content
beyond the viewport. Evaluate logical flow appropriate to the writing system, such as top-to-bottom
and left-to-right in LTR content. Do not assume one reading direction or scrolling method across
languages and input configurations.

**AT Mechanisms**: Visual reading, Mouse wheel / scroll gesture **Device context**: All

**UI Requirements**: Text must be readable: sufficient contrast, reasonable font size, adequate line
spacing. Content should not require horizontal scrolling at standard viewport widths. Long pages
should provide navigation aids (table of contents, back-to-top).

### Form interaction _(from shared)_

Form tasks may involve reaching fields, typing, selecting options, checking boxes, and submitting
through different input methods. Evaluate clear labels, placeholder text that does not replace
labels, and actionable error feedback without assuming pointer access.

**AT Mechanisms**: Keyboard (typing), Mouse (clicking fields), Visual reading (labels) **Device
context**: All

**UI Requirements**: Every form field must have a visible label. Required fields must be clearly
indicated. Error messages must be specific, adjacent to the field, and visible without scrolling.
Successful submission must be clearly confirmed.

### Navigation and wayfinding _(from shared)_

Menus, breadcrumbs, links, and browser back/forward can support navigation between pages and views.
Evaluate whether the current location, available destinations, and return paths are understandable
through the access strategies in scope.

**AT Mechanisms**: Visual scanning, Mouse clicking, Browser controls **Device context**: All

**UI Requirements**: Consistent navigation across pages. Current location clearly indicated (active
nav item, breadcrumbs). Important actions reachable from any page. Browser back button should work
predictably.

### Touch / pointer interaction _(from shared)_

Touch interaction may include taps, swipes, pinch-zoom, or long presses, depending on the device and
access method. Direct touch has no hover preview; evaluate discoverable controls, target size, and
alternatives to complex gestures. Do not require every touch interaction pattern to be available to
the same person.

**AT Mechanisms**: Touchscreen tap, Swipe gestures, Pinch-zoom **Device context**: Mobile

**UI Requirements**: Touch targets are easy to activate without accidentally activating adjacent
controls — small or tightly packed targets force users to retry or hit the wrong control. No
reliance on hover for essential information. Custom gestures must have single-pointer alternatives.
Drag-and-drop must have tap-based alternatives. No accidental activation from scroll gestures.

## Interaction Contexts

### User with achromatopsia (complete colour blindness)

When no colour differentiation is available, including contexts relevant to achromatopsia, check all
meaning without hue. Greyscale is a useful evaluation condition, not a simulation of anyone's
complete experience. Reduced acuity and light sensitivity may coexist; this FPS lens focuses on
colour without declaring those needs absent.

**Primary AT**: None assumed for this colour-independent check **Device context**: All **Preferred
navigation**: Scanning for non-colour cues, Seeking explicit labels and legends, Interpreting state
and feedback without colour

**Expectations**:

- No information conveyed through colour alone
- All colour-coded content has a non-colour alternative (text, icon, pattern, shape)
- Charts and graphs use patterns or direct labels, not colour legend alone
- Error/success/warning states include icons or text, not just colour
- Links are visually distinct from body text without relying on colour
- Sufficient contrast between all foreground and background elements

### User with protanopia (red-blind) or deuteranopia (green-blind)

Where red/green differentiation is unreliable, including protanopia or deuteranopia contexts, check
error/success indicators, traffic-light metaphors, and red-green heatmaps. Reds, greens, browns, and
oranges may be confused; do not assume other colours are reliably distinguished. The source's
approximate ~8% of males prevalence figure describes population context, not an individual's
perception.

**Primary AT**: Windows Colour Filters, macOS Colour Filters **Device context**: All **Preferred
navigation**: Scanning for non-colour cues, Interpreting state and feedback without colour,
Identifying interactive elements by shape and affordance

**Expectations**:

- Red/green colour pairs are never the sole differentiator
- Error (red) and success (green) states include icons or text
- Charts don't rely on red-green differentiation without patterns
- Colour palettes avoid red/green adjacency for categorical data
- Status indicators use shape, icon, or text in addition to colour

### User with tritanopia (blue-yellow blind)

Where blue/yellow differentiation is unreliable, including the rarer tritanopia context, check
blue/yellow interface coding and gradients. Blues, yellows, and greens may be confused; do not
assume reliable red/green perception or identical distinctions for everyone.

**Primary AT**: Windows Colour Filters, macOS Colour Filters **Device context**: All **Preferred
navigation**: Scanning for non-colour cues, Seeking explicit labels and legends

**Expectations**:

- Blue/yellow colour pairs are not the sole differentiator
- Information links (often styled blue) have underline or other non-colour treatment
- Warning indicators (often yellow) include icon or text
- Charts using blue-yellow gradients have pattern or label alternatives

### New / infrequent user _(from shared)_

First-time or infrequent interaction may require rediscovering controls and workflows. Labels,
accessible affordances, and progressive disclosure can support learning without requiring prior
knowledge of shortcuts or hidden features.

**Primary AT**: None **Device context**: All **Preferred navigation**: Visual scanning and layout
comprehension, Navigation and wayfinding, Form interaction

**Expectations**:

- Interface is learnable without documentation
- Primary actions are visually obvious
- Error messages guide toward resolution
- Navigation structure is understandable

### Expert / frequent user _(from shared)_

Frequent interaction may build familiarity with layouts, shortcuts, and workflows. Consistency and
efficient paths can support established strategies; changes may require relearning. Expertise in a
task does not establish expertise with every assistive technology.

**Primary AT**: None **Device context**: All **Preferred navigation**: Mouse / pointer interaction,
Navigation and wayfinding

**Expectations**:

- Keyboard shortcuts for common tasks
- Consistent layout across sessions and pages
- Efficient paths to frequently used features
- Changes to familiar workflows are clearly communicated

## Learning Considerations

- **First visit**: Check whether unfamiliar status, categories, and controls reveal their meaning
  without colour. A colour-only cue may obstruct first-use understanding; non-colour differentiation
  does not establish that all other access requirements are met.

- **Colour is contextual**: The same colour can mean different things in different contexts (red =
  error in forms, red = negative in finance, red = hot in temperature maps). The user needs the
  MEANING stated explicitly, not just the colour changed.

- **Colour filters help but don't solve**: OS colour filters can remap colours to make differences
  more visible, but they cannot add information that isn't there. If the only difference between two
  states is hue, no filter can make that difference semantic.

- **Dark mode and theming**: Users may prefer high-contrast or dark themes that reduce the reliance
  on subtle colour differences. However, if the interface encodes meaning in colour (e.g., coloured
  badges, status dots), the problem persists regardless of theme.

- **First visit** _(from shared)_: Familiar web, desktop, and mobile conventions may support
  learning, but recognition of hamburger menus, search icons, tabs, or modals is not guaranteed.
  Explain unfamiliar patterns and expose their purpose through accessible labels and feedback.

- **Mental models** _(from shared)_: Early interactions may establish expectations. Inconsistent
  behavior, such as a button that sometimes navigates and sometimes submits, can undermine
  confidence and require additional checking.

- **Error recovery** _(from shared)_: Support recovery from mistakes through undo, appropriate
  confirmation dialogs, and clear error messages. Make the consequences of irreversible actions
  clear rather than assuming every mistake can be avoided.

- **Cognitive load** _(from shared)_: Clutter, excessive options, and unclear hierarchy can increase
  attention and memory demands. Evaluate those demands in the task context without inferring a
  person's cognitive capabilities.

## Heuristic Lens Overrides

### Heuristic 1 — Visibility of System Status

System status must not rely on colour alone. Check loading, progress, and confirmations that change
only in colour (e.g., a bar turning green) for meaningful text, icons, or numeric/textual progress
rather than hue alone.

### Heuristic 2 — Match Between System and Real World

Red = danger, green = go, and yellow = caution need non-colour meaning. Check category labels,
status icons, and chart legends. A green checkmark vs. red X is not colour-alone when shape conveys
the distinction; flag only where the remaining shapes or labels are insufficient.

### Heuristic 3 — User Control and Freedom

Undo, cancel, and destructive actions are often colour-coded (red for delete, grey for cancel). If
the only difference between 'Delete' and 'Cancel' buttons is colour, the user may activate the wrong
one. Look for: destructive action buttons distinguished only by colour, not by text, icon, or
position.

### Heuristic 4 — Consistency and Standards

Check consistent non-colour indicators across similar components. Switching from icons + colour to
colour alone removes the alternative cue and can disrupt a learned pattern.

### Heuristic 5 — Error Prevention

Error prevention messages and constraints should not rely on colour to communicate. Required fields
marked only with a red asterisk (if the asterisk is not also accompanied by text like 'required')
are problematic only if the colour is the differentiator from decorative asterisks. Look for:
validation indicators that use colour alone.

### Heuristic 6 — Recognition Rather than Recall

Recognition is impaired when colour is the organizing principle. Colour-coded tabs, categories, or
navigation where the colour IS the label (e.g., a 'red team' vs. 'blue team' with no text) force
recall. Look for: colour as the sole categorization or labeling mechanism.

### Heuristic 7 — Flexibility and Efficiency of Use

Check colour-coded filters, sort indicators, and selection states for readily available non-colour
cues. Seeking a separate alternative can add effort. Keyboard shortcuts are evaluated separately,
not presumed usable; shortcuts cannot compensate for undiscoverable state.

### Heuristic 8 — Aesthetic and Minimalist Design

Check whether minimalism replaces status text with colour-only dots and thereby removes
colour-independent information. Retain meaningful text, icons, or shapes rather than indicators
whose meaning depends only on hue.

### Heuristic 9 — Help Users Recognize, Diagnose, and Recover from Errors

Error messages must be identifiable without relying on red text or red borders as the sole
indicator. Look for: error states that use only colour change (red border, red text) without an icon
(⚠, ✗), prefix text ('Error:'), or other non-colour marker. Success states that use only green
without a checkmark or 'Success' text.

### Heuristic 10 — Help and Documentation

Check instructions such as 'click the green button' or 'errors are shown in red' for additional
labels, position, or meaningful icons. Colour-only references fail this lens.
