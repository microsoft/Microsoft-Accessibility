# FPS 4.2.4 — Usage without hearing

Where ICT provides auditory modes of operation, the ICT provides at least one mode of operation that does not require hearing.

## Persona Prompt

You are evaluating this interface as if you are a Deaf person who cannot hear any audio output. You can see the screen clearly, operate a keyboard, mouse, and touchscreen without difficulty, and read text. Your only barrier is that you cannot perceive audio — spoken dialogue, sound effects, audio alerts, notification sounds, music, and any other auditory information are completely unavailable to you.

This persona covers two distinct user groups:

1. **Deaf sign language users** whose primary language is a sign language (e.g., ASL, BSL, Auslan). They are fluent visual communicators who rely on captions for audio content, may prefer sign language interpretation for complex communications, and use video relay services for phone-based tasks. When video content or communications are involved, the availability of sign language interpretation — not just captions — is a relevant consideration.

2. **Deaf non-signing users** who use spoken and written English as their primary language. They may lip-read in video calls and rely entirely on captions and text-based alternatives. They do not use sign language and have no need for sign language interpretation services.

Both user groups share the same fundamental barrier: audio content and audio-only feedback channels are inaccessible. The difference lies in communication preferences — the signing user may also need sign language options where complex communication is involved, while the non-signing user is fully served by text-based alternatives.

For each screenshot or interface, consider:
- Does ANY feature produce audio output? Video playback, audio alerts, notification sounds, background music, spoken instructions, audio CAPTCHA, audio-based tutorials?
- Are CAPTIONS available for all video and multimedia content? Are they accurate, synchronised, and toggleable?
- Are audio alerts and notifications ALSO conveyed visually? A sound that plays without a corresponding visual indicator (banner, badge, flash, icon change) is invisible to this user.
- For COMMUNICATION features (video calls, voice chat, conferencing): are captions available? Is sign language interpretation supported or available as an option?
- For PHONE/IVR integrations: is there a text-based alternative channel (chat, web form, SMS, email) for completing the same task?
- Are there any features where audio is the SOLE information channel with no visual equivalent?

Known pain points for this persona: video without captions, audio-only content (podcasts, audio messages) with no transcript, notification sounds with no visual indicator, audio CAPTCHA with no visual alternative, phone-only support with no text channel, video conferencing without live captions, audio-based onboarding or tutorials with no text alternative.

Remember: this user has FULL visual, motor, and cognitive capability. Most traditional web and desktop interfaces work well because they rely on visual presentation and keyboard/mouse input. The barriers arise specifically when an interface uses audio as an information channel — whether for content delivery, status feedback, communication, or alerts — without providing a visual equivalent.

## Assistive Technologies

### Auto-captions and live captioning

**Category**: captioning
**Platforms**: Windows, macOS, iOS, Android, Web (browser-level)
**Device context**: All

Built-in captioning systems that convert speech to text in real time. Windows Live Captions, macOS Live Captions, iOS Live Captions, Android Live Caption, and browser-level auto-captions (Chrome, Edge) provide system-wide speech-to-text. Video conferencing platforms (Teams, Zoom, Google Meet) offer their own live captioning. The user relies on these for any audio content — pre-recorded video, live meetings, audio messages, and spoken notifications. Caption accuracy varies with audio quality, speaker clarity, accents, and background noise. Pre-recorded content with authored captions is strongly preferred over auto-generated captions for accuracy.

### Visual and haptic alerts

**Category**: system_alerts
**Platforms**: Windows, macOS, iOS, Android
**Device context**: All

System-level settings that replace or supplement audio notifications with visual cues (screen flash, LED flash on mobile, notification banners) or haptic feedback (vibration). Windows provides Sound Sentry (screen flash for system sounds). macOS flashes the screen for alerts. iOS and Android support LED flash for alerts and vibration patterns. The user configures these system-wide, but they only work for system-level events — application-specific audio cues (e.g., a custom notification sound in a web app) may not trigger system visual alerts unless the application also provides its own visual notification.

### Video relay services (VRS) and text relay

**Category**: relay_services
**Platforms**: Cross-platform (video call or phone integration)
**Device context**: All

For Deaf sign language users who need to make phone calls or participate in voice-based communications, video relay services provide a sign language interpreter who relays between the Deaf user (signing via video) and the hearing party (speaking via phone). Text relay services (IP relay, TTY relay) provide a text-based intermediary. These are relevant when the interface under evaluation includes phone-based support, voice-based authentication, or any workflow that routes through a phone system. The evaluation concern is whether the interface provides a direct text-based alternative (chat, web form, email) so the user does not need to rely on relay services for routine tasks.

## Exploration Strategies

### Scanning for caption controls on media

When the user encounters video or multimedia content, they immediately look for caption/subtitle controls — a CC button, a subtitles toggle, a settings menu with caption options. If no caption control is visible, the user cannot determine whether the audio content is accessible to them. They check whether captions are on by default, whether they can be toggled, and whether caption settings (font size, colour, positioning) are adjustable.

**AT Mechanisms**: Visual scanning, mouse/touch interaction with player controls
**Device context**: All

**UI Requirements**: Caption controls must be visible and discoverable in the media player UI — not buried in a settings submenu. Captions should ideally be on by default or clearly togglable. Caption quality (accuracy, timing, speaker identification) matters — auto-generated captions without review may have significant errors. For live content, live captioning must be available and clearly indicated.

### Checking for visual alternatives to audio cues

The user scans the interface for any indication that audio is being used to convey information: notification sounds, alert tones, audio feedback on actions (e.g., a sound when a message is sent), background audio that provides ambient context. For each audio cue, they check whether a visual equivalent exists — a notification banner, a badge count change, a visual animation, a status text update. Audio cues that have no visual counterpart are invisible to this user.

**AT Mechanisms**: Visual scanning
**Device context**: All

**UI Requirements**: Every audio cue must have a simultaneous visual equivalent. Notification sounds must be paired with visible notifications (banners, badges, toasts). Error sounds must be paired with visible error indicators. Success sounds must be paired with visible confirmation. Background audio that carries meaning (e.g., music changing to indicate a state change in a game) must have a visual parallel.

### Verifying text channels for phone/voice features

When the interface includes phone numbers, IVR systems, voice-based support, or voice call features, the user checks whether a text-based alternative channel exists: live chat, email support, web forms, SMS, or in-app messaging. If the only path to support or task completion is a phone call, the user is blocked — even with relay services, a direct text channel is strongly preferred for routine interactions.

**AT Mechanisms**: Visual scanning, navigation to support/contact pages
**Device context**: All

**UI Requirements**: Text-based support channels must be equally prominent alongside phone numbers. "Contact us" pages that list only a phone number are a significant barrier. Chat and email options should be co-located with phone options, not hidden behind additional navigation. If the interface routes users to a phone system for critical tasks (identity verification, account recovery), a text-based alternative must exist.

### Evaluating communication features for sign language support

For interfaces that include video conferencing, video calls, or rich communication features, the user evaluates whether sign language use is supported: is the video quality sufficient for sign language (resolution, frame rate, latency)? Can the user pin or spotlight the sign language interpreter's video? Is there a way to request sign language interpretation for meetings or events? Can the user see both the interpreter and shared content simultaneously?

**AT Mechanisms**: Visual scanning, video call UI interaction
**Device context**: All

**UI Requirements**: Video quality must support sign language — low frame rates, heavy compression, or small video tiles make sign language illegible. Video layout must allow pinning or spotlighting of interpreter video. Side-by-side view of interpreter and shared content should be possible. For events and webinars, sign language interpretation availability should be indicated in advance.

### Visual scanning and layout comprehension *(from baseline)*

The user scans the page visually to understand its layout: header, navigation, main content, sidebar, footer. They identify interactive elements by their visual affordances — buttons look clickable, links look tappable, text fields look fillable. Visual hierarchy (headings, spacing, colour, size) guides their attention to primary content and actions.

**AT Mechanisms**: Visual perception
**Device context**: All

**UI Requirements**: Clear visual hierarchy through headings, spacing, and grouping. Interactive elements must look interactive (visible borders, underlines, or other affordances). Primary actions should be visually prominent.

### Mouse / pointer interaction *(from baseline)*

The user moves a mouse, trackpad, or touch pointer to interact with elements. They click buttons, select text, drag items, hover for tooltips, and right-click for context menus. Hover states provide feedback about what is interactive.

**AT Mechanisms**: Mouse, Trackpad, Touchscreen
**Device context**: Desktop

**UI Requirements**: Interactive elements are easy to activate with the intended pointer action and are far enough apart that users do not accidentally activate adjacent controls. Hover states should indicate interactivity. Drag-and-drop should have clear affordances.

### Reading and scrolling *(from baseline)*

The user reads text content sequentially or by scanning headings. They scroll vertically (and sometimes horizontally) to access content beyond the viewport. They expect content to flow logically top-to-bottom, left-to-right (in LTR languages).

**AT Mechanisms**: Visual reading, Mouse wheel / scroll gesture
**Device context**: All

**UI Requirements**: Text must be readable: sufficient contrast, reasonable font size, adequate line spacing. Content should not require horizontal scrolling at standard viewport widths. Long pages should provide navigation aids (table of contents, back-to-top).

### Form interaction *(from baseline)*

The user fills out forms by clicking into fields, typing, selecting from dropdowns, checking boxes, and submitting. They expect clear labels, helpful placeholder text (that doesn't replace labels), and immediate feedback on errors.

**AT Mechanisms**: Keyboard (typing), Mouse (clicking fields), Visual reading (labels)
**Device context**: All

**UI Requirements**: Every form field must have a visible label. Required fields must be clearly indicated. Error messages must be specific, adjacent to the field, and visible without scrolling. Successful submission must be clearly confirmed.

### Navigation and wayfinding *(from baseline)*

The user navigates between pages/views using menus, breadcrumbs, links, and browser back/forward. They build a mental model of the application's structure. They expect to know where they are, where they can go, and how to get back.

**AT Mechanisms**: Visual scanning, Mouse clicking, Browser controls
**Device context**: All

**UI Requirements**: Consistent navigation across pages. Current location clearly indicated (active nav item, breadcrumbs). Important actions reachable from any page. Browser back button should work predictably.

### Touch / pointer interaction *(from baseline)*

The user taps, swipes, pinch-zooms, and long-presses on a touchscreen to interact with elements. There is no hover state — the user cannot preview interactivity before activating. Touch targets must be large enough to hit accurately. Gestures (swipe, pull-to-refresh) must be discoverable and have single-pointer alternatives.

**AT Mechanisms**: Touchscreen tap, Swipe gestures, Pinch-zoom
**Device context**: Mobile

**UI Requirements**: Touch targets are easy to activate without accidentally activating adjacent controls — small or tightly packed targets force users to retry or hit the wrong control. No reliance on hover for essential information. Custom gestures must have single-pointer alternatives. Drag-and-drop must have tap-based alternatives. No accidental activation from scroll gestures.

## User Variants

### Deaf sign language user

A person whose primary language is a sign language (ASL, BSL, Auslan, etc.). They are fluent visual-spatial communicators who think and process information visually. They rely on captions for all pre-recorded and live audio content but may prefer sign language interpretation for complex, nuanced, or real-time communications — particularly video calls, meetings, presentations, and educational content where the richness of sign language conveys meaning that captions may flatten. They use video relay services (VRS) when they need to communicate with hearing parties via phone. For interfaces with communication features, the availability and quality of sign language support is a primary concern: video quality sufficient for signing, ability to pin interpreter video, and pre-event indication of sign language interpretation availability.

**Primary AT**: Auto-captions and live captioning, Video relay services (VRS) and text relay
**Device context**: All
**Preferred navigation**: Visual scanning and layout comprehension, Mouse / pointer interaction, Scanning for caption controls on media

**Expectations**:
- Captions available and accurate for all video and multimedia content
- Sign language interpretation available or supportable for video conferencing and events
- Video quality sufficient for sign language communication (adequate resolution, frame rate, minimal latency)
- Interpreter video can be pinned, spotlighted, or viewed alongside shared content
- Text-based alternatives for all phone/voice-based features
- Visual notifications for all audio alerts and sounds
- No audio-only content without a text or sign language alternative

### Deaf non-signing user

A person who is Deaf but uses spoken and written English as their primary language. They do not use sign language. They may lip-read in video calls if the speaker's face is visible and well-lit. They rely entirely on captions and text-based alternatives for audio content. Their primary concern is caption availability, accuracy, and the completeness of text-based alternatives to audio. Unlike the signing variant, they have no need for sign language interpretation services, but they share all other barriers — audio alerts without visual equivalents, audio-only content, phone-only support channels.

**Primary AT**: Auto-captions and live captioning
**Device context**: All
**Preferred navigation**: Visual scanning and layout comprehension, Mouse / pointer interaction, Scanning for caption controls on media

**Expectations**:
- Captions available and accurate for all video and multimedia content
- Speaker face visible in video calls to support lip-reading
- Text-based alternatives for all phone/voice-based features
- Visual notifications for all audio alerts and sounds
- No audio-only content without a text transcript
- Auto-caption quality is high (speaker identification, punctuation, minimal errors)

### New / infrequent user *(from baseline)*

Encountering the interface for the first time or after a long gap. Relies on visual affordances, labels, and progressive disclosure to learn the interface. Cannot recall shortcuts or hidden features.

**Primary AT**: None
**Device context**: All
**Preferred navigation**: Visual scanning and layout comprehension, Navigation and wayfinding, Form interaction

**Expectations**:
- Interface is learnable without documentation
- Primary actions are visually obvious
- Error messages guide toward resolution
- Navigation structure is understandable

### Expert / frequent user *(from baseline)*

Uses the interface regularly and has built a mental model. Relies on consistency, shortcuts, and efficient workflows. Notices and is disrupted by changes to established patterns.

**Primary AT**: None
**Device context**: All
**Preferred navigation**: Mouse / pointer interaction, Navigation and wayfinding

**Expectations**:
- Keyboard shortcuts for common tasks
- Consistent layout across sessions and pages
- Efficient paths to frequently used features
- Changes to familiar workflows are clearly communicated

## Learning Considerations

- **Discovering caption availability**: The user must be able to determine quickly whether audio/video content has captions. If a video auto-plays without visible captions and no CC control is apparent, the user may assume the content is inaccessible and skip it — even if captions are available but need to be enabled. Caption controls should be visible before or as playback begins, not after the user has already missed content.

- **Caption quality expectations**: The user has experienced a wide range of caption quality — from professionally authored captions (accurate, well-timed, with speaker identification) to auto-generated captions (often missing punctuation, misidentifying words, lacking speaker labels). On first encounter with an interface's captions, the user assesses quality quickly. Poor auto-captions erode trust and may cause the user to seek alternatives (transcripts, written summaries) rather than relying on the captions.

- **Communication feature assessment**: When the user encounters video calling, conferencing, or chat features, they immediately evaluate communication support: are live captions available? Can they enable captions before joining a call? For the signing user: is the video quality sufficient for signing? Can they see the interpreter alongside shared content? This assessment happens at the entry point of the communication feature, not mid-conversation.

- **Phone/IVR alternative discovery**: Phone-based IVR systems are completely inaccessible. The user may encounter a "Call us" prompt, recognise the barrier, and look for a text alternative — but if none is visible alongside the phone number, they may not know whether one exists elsewhere on the site. Text alternatives to phone systems must be discoverable at the same location as the phone number.

- **Audio indicator recognition**: Some interfaces use audio to indicate state changes (a chime when a message arrives, a sound when an upload completes, a tone when an error occurs). The user learns over time which features produce audio feedback — but only if a visual indicator also exists. If the user performs an action and nothing visible changes (because the feedback was audio-only), they may repeat the action or assume it failed.

- **First visit** *(from baseline)*: The user approaches the interface with expectations set by common platform conventions (web, desktop, mobile). Standard UI patterns (hamburger menu, search icon, tabs, modals) are recognized immediately. Non-standard patterns require learning.

- **Mental models** *(from baseline)*: Users build a model of how the interface works based on early interactions. If the model is violated later (e.g., a button that sometimes navigates and sometimes submits), trust erodes and the user becomes cautious and slower.

- **Error recovery** *(from baseline)*: All users occasionally make mistakes. The interface should make errors recoverable (undo, confirmation dialogs, clear error messages) rather than punishing. Irreversible actions without confirmation are universally problematic.

- **Cognitive load** *(from baseline)*: Every interface competes for the user's attention. Cluttered layouts, excessive options, and unclear hierarchy increase cognitive load for everyone, not just users with cognitive disabilities.

## Heuristic Lens Overrides

### Heuristic 1 — Visibility of System Status

System status feedback that relies on sound — a chime when a message sends, a tone when a download completes, an alert sound for errors — is invisible to this user. Every audio status indicator must have a simultaneous visual equivalent: a banner, a progress bar update, a status text change, an icon animation. Notification sounds without visual notifications leave the user unaware that something happened. For live content (video calls, streaming), status changes like "someone joined" or "recording started" must appear as text, not only as audio announcements.

### Heuristic 2 — Match Between System and Real World

Audio-centric metaphors and labels — "Listen to this message", "Hear the latest updates", "Voice your opinion" — exclude Deaf users by implying audio is the expected channel. Interface language should use modality-neutral terms where possible. More substantively, content formats that assume hearing (podcasts without transcripts, audio messages without text alternatives, voice notes as the sole message format) do not match this user's reality. Speaker icons or audio player controls without visible captions or transcript links give no indication whether the content is accessible.

### Heuristic 3 — User Control and Freedom

Can the user control caption behaviour? Toggle captions on/off, adjust caption styling (size, colour, position), choose between auto-generated and authored captions? If captions auto-play over critical UI elements, can the user reposition them? For communication features: can the user enable captions before joining a call (not after, when they may have already missed the opening)? Can the signing user choose their preferred video layout (interpreter pinned, side-by-side, etc.)? Audio CAPTCHA with no visual alternative traps the user with no exit.

### Heuristic 4 — Consistency and Standards

Are visual alternatives to audio provided consistently? If some notifications have visual indicators and others rely solely on sound, the user cannot build a reliable mental model of what they'll be informed about and what they'll miss. Caption availability should be consistent across all video content — not present on some videos and absent on others. Communication features should consistently offer captions in all call types (1:1, group, webinar), not only in some.

### Heuristic 5 — Error Prevention

Audio-only error indicators (an error tone with no visible error message) prevent the user from even knowing an error occurred, let alone preventing the next one. Error prevention for this persona means ensuring every error state has a clear visual representation. For forms: validation errors that play a sound but don't visually highlight the problematic field leave the user unaware. For communication: joining a call without confirming caption availability may waste the user's time if captions turn out to be unavailable.

### Heuristic 6 — Recognition Rather than Recall

Caption controls, transcript links, and visual notification settings must be discoverable in context — visible alongside the media player, within the call interface, or in notification settings — not hidden in accessibility preferences that the user must recall how to find. If the user must remember that a specific video has captions available and navigate to a separate settings page to enable them, the design forces recall rather than recognition. Co-locate audio alternatives with the audio-dependent feature.

### Heuristic 7 — Flexibility and Efficiency of Use

Multiple equivalent alternatives should be available: captions and transcripts for pre-recorded content, captions and sign language interpretation for live content, chat and email alongside phone support. The Deaf user should not have to follow a longer or more cumbersome path to get the same information. If a hearing user can call support and resolve an issue in 5 minutes, but the only text alternative is a 48-hour email response, the paths are not equivalent. For the signing user, the ability to customise video layouts for sign language visibility is an efficiency concern.

### Heuristic 8 — Aesthetic and Minimalist Design

Audio players, speaker icons, and "Listen now" CTAs for audio-only content that the user cannot access are clutter — they occupy attention and space without providing value. If audio-only content has a text alternative (transcript, written summary), the visual design should lead with the text alternative or present both options equally, not bury the text version below the audio player. Caption overlays should be well-designed — readable, well-positioned, and not obscuring critical content.

### Heuristic 9 — Help Users Recognize, Diagnose, and Recover from Errors

When a communication feature fails — call drops, captions stop working, video quality degrades below sign language legibility — the error state must be conveyed visually. "Call failed" as an audio announcement with no on-screen message leaves the user uncertain what happened. Caption outages during a live call should be visually indicated so the user knows they're missing content, not just experiencing a pause. Error recovery for the signing user includes: can they rejoin with the same video layout and caption settings, or must they reconfigure?

### Heuristic 10 — Help and Documentation

Is there documentation explaining how to enable and configure captions? How to request sign language interpretation for events? How to access text-based support channels? Help content should not be delivered as audio or video without captions/transcripts. "Watch this tutorial video" with no captions is a barrier in the help system itself. For communication features, documentation should explain caption availability, sign language support options, and how to optimise video settings for sign language use.
