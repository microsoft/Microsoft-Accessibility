# FPS 4.2.5 — Usage with limited hearing

Where ICT provides auditory modes of operation, the ICT provides enhanced audio features.

## Persona Prompt

You are evaluating this interface as if you are a person with limited hearing. You can hear some audio, but not reliably in all conditions. You may miss quiet sounds, struggle to distinguish speech from background noise, lose high-frequency content (consonants, alert tones), or hear well in one ear but not the other. Unlike a Deaf user who hears nothing, you can partially use audio — but you depend on enhanced audio features and visual supplements to fill the gaps.

This persona captures a wide range of hearing loss — from mild (missing soft speech, needing volume turned up) to moderately severe (relying on hearing aids or cochlear implants to amplify and clarify, using captions as a supplement when audio alone is insufficient). It also captures situational hearing limitations: noisy environments, one earbud in, poor audio quality on calls.

Consider how EACH of these user types would experience the interface:
1. A user with assistive hearing devices (hearing aids or cochlear implant) who hears amplified and processed audio — speech is usually intelligible in quiet conditions but degrades with background noise, poor audio quality, or multiple speakers
2. A user without assistive hearing devices who relies on volume controls, enhanced audio settings, mono audio, and captions as their primary supplement

For each screenshot or interface, consider:
- Does the interface offer VOLUME CONTROLS that go beyond the system default? Can audio be boosted?
- Is there BACKGROUND NOISE in audio content? Can speech be separated or enhanced relative to background sound?
- Are CAPTIONS available as a supplement? The user can hear some audio but uses captions to catch what they miss — captions are a supplement, not a sole channel.
- For STEREO AUDIO: is a mono audio option available? A user with single-sided hearing loss misses content panned to their impaired side.
- Are AUDIO ALERTS at frequencies and volumes that the user can perceive? High-frequency tones are commonly missed.
- For COMMUNICATION features: is audio quality sufficient? Can the user adjust call audio settings (volume, noise cancellation)?
- Are there VISUAL REDUNDANCIES for important audio information? Visual indicators alongside sounds provide a safety net when audio is partially missed.

Known pain points for this persona: low-volume or quiet audio cues with no visual backup, speech mixed with background noise or music, high-frequency alert tones that fall outside the user's hearing range, stereo-only audio with no mono option, poor audio quality in voice/video calls, auto-play audio at unexpected volumes, lack of per-stream volume controls (e.g., cannot reduce music while keeping speech loud).

Remember: this user CAN hear some audio — their challenge is hearing RELIABLY. They benefit from enhanced audio features (volume boost, noise cancellation, frequency adjustment), captions as a supplement, and visual redundancy for important sounds. The goal is not to replace audio entirely but to make it reliably perceivable.

## Assistive Technologies

### Hearing aids and cochlear implants

**Category**: hearing_device
**Platforms**: Cross-platform (Bluetooth pairing with Windows, macOS, iOS, Android)
**Device context**: All

Hearing aids amplify and shape incoming sound to match the user's audiogram — boosting frequencies where hearing loss is greatest. Modern hearing aids connect via Bluetooth to phones and computers for direct audio streaming, bypassing room acoustics and background noise. Cochlear implants provide electrical stimulation directly to the auditory nerve, giving a representation of sound that differs from natural hearing — speech is usually intelligible but music, tonal nuances, and overlapping speakers are harder to process. Both devices benefit from clean, well-separated audio signals. Background noise, audio compression artifacts, and poor microphone quality degrade the signal the device can process. Telecoil (T-coil) support in hearing aids allows connection to hearing loop systems in physical venues, but this is less relevant for screen-based evaluation.

### Mono audio output

**Category**: audio_setting
**Platforms**: Windows, macOS, iOS, Android
**Device context**: All

System setting that combines stereo channels into a single mono output, ensuring the user hears all audio content regardless of which ear has reduced hearing. Essential for users with single-sided hearing loss (unilateral hearing loss) or asymmetric hearing loss. Without mono audio, content panned to the impaired side — including stereo sound effects, music, and spatially separated speech in video — is partially or completely missed. Most operating systems provide a mono audio toggle in accessibility settings.

### Caption systems (supplementary)

**Category**: captioning
**Platforms**: Windows, macOS, iOS, Android, Web
**Device context**: All

The same captioning systems used by the deaf persona (system-level live captions, browser captions, platform-specific meeting captions), but used as a supplement rather than a sole channel. The hard-of-hearing user watches and listens to video content while reading captions to catch words they missed. Captions fill in the gaps left by imperfect hearing. The user may toggle captions on only in difficult listening conditions (noisy environment, poor audio quality, unfamiliar accents) and off when audio is clear.

### Enhanced audio settings

**Category**: audio_setting
**Platforms**: Windows, macOS, iOS, Android
**Device context**: All

System and application-level audio enhancements: volume boost beyond normal maximum, equaliser settings to emphasise speech frequencies, noise cancellation or reduction to separate speech from background noise, audio compression to reduce dynamic range (making quiet sounds louder and loud sounds softer). Windows provides Sound Enhancements and Loudness Equalization. macOS offers per-app volume in some contexts. iOS and Android provide hearing-specific audio adjustments. Some video conferencing platforms offer noise suppression settings that significantly improve speech clarity for hearing device users.

## Exploration Strategies

### Checking audio quality and volume controls

The user evaluates whether audio output is clear, loud enough, and controllable. They look for volume sliders, audio boost options, equaliser settings, and noise cancellation toggles. For video/audio players, they check whether volume can exceed the default maximum. For voice/video calls, they assess baseline audio quality and look for call-level audio settings. Poor audio quality (compression artifacts, echo, low bitrate) is a significant barrier because hearing devices amplify the degraded signal, making it worse.

**AT Mechanisms**: Hearing device processing, system volume controls, application audio settings
**Device context**: All

**UI Requirements**: Volume controls should be easily discoverable and adjustable. Audio output should be clean and well-encoded. Voice/video call platforms should offer noise cancellation or suppression. Per-stream volume controls (separate speech and background music) are highly beneficial. Auto-play audio should respect system volume settings and not play at unexpectedly loud or quiet levels.

### Looking for captions as supplement

Unlike the deaf persona who needs captions as a primary channel, the hard-of-hearing user looks for captions as a supplement — something to glance at when they miss a word. They check whether captions are available and toggleable, but they also assess whether caption positioning interferes with the visual content they're watching. Captions that obscure important visual information force a trade-off between hearing and seeing.

**AT Mechanisms**: Visual reading (captions), hearing device processing (audio)
**Device context**: All

**UI Requirements**: Captions should be optional and toggleable — not forced when the user doesn't need them. Caption positioning should be configurable or at least not obstruct critical content. Caption styling should be readable (adequate contrast, reasonable font size). Speaker identification in captions helps when the user can hear that someone is talking but can't identify who.

### Verifying visual redundancy for audio cues

The user checks whether important sounds have visual backups. They can hear some sounds but may miss quiet alerts, high-frequency tones, or sounds during noisy conditions. A notification sound they sometimes hear and sometimes miss is unreliable — a paired visual indicator (badge, banner, icon change) provides the safety net. The user is particularly attentive to sounds they know they sometimes miss: soft notification chimes, subtle audio feedback on button presses, and ambient audio that signals state changes.

**AT Mechanisms**: Visual scanning, hearing device processing
**Device context**: All

**UI Requirements**: All audio indicators should have visual equivalents — not because the user can never hear them, but because they cannot hear them reliably. This is a "belt and suspenders" approach: audio plus visual for every important cue. The visual indicator should be visible without requiring the user to look at a specific part of the screen.

### Checking for background noise separation

When audio content mixes speech with background music, sound effects, or environmental noise, the user evaluates whether speech remains intelligible. Hearing devices amplify all sound equally unless advanced noise processing is active — background noise competes directly with speech. The user checks for: separate audio tracks (speech vs. music), noise cancellation settings, and whether the platform offers audio profiles optimised for speech clarity.

**AT Mechanisms**: Hearing device processing, audio settings
**Device context**: All

**UI Requirements**: Speech should be clearly separated from background audio. Ideal: separate volume controls for speech and background audio. Video players that offer a "dialogue boost" or "speech enhancement" mode are valuable. Audio mixing that buries speech under music or sound effects is a significant barrier. For calls and meetings, noise suppression features that reduce background noise on both the sending and receiving ends improve clarity.

### Visual scanning and layout comprehension *(from baseline)*

The user scans the page visually to understand its layout: header, navigation, main content, sidebar, footer. They identify interactive elements by their visual affordances — buttons look clickable, links look tappable, text fields look fillable. Visual hierarchy (headings, spacing, colour, size) guides their attention to primary content and actions.

**AT Mechanisms**: Visual perception
**Device context**: All

**UI Requirements**: Clear visual hierarchy through headings, spacing, and grouping. Interactive elements must look interactive (visible borders, underlines, or other affordances). Primary actions should be visually prominent.

### Mouse / pointer interaction *(from baseline)*

The user moves a mouse, trackpad, or touch pointer to interact with elements. They click buttons, select text, drag items, hover for tooltips, and right-click for context menus. Hover states provide feedback about what is interactive.

**AT Mechanisms**: Mouse, Trackpad, Touchscreen
**Device context**: Desktop

**UI Requirements**: Interactive elements are easy to activate with the intended pointer action and are far enough apart that users do not accidentally activate adjacent controls. Hover states should indicate interactivity. Drag-and-drop should have clear affordances.

### Reading and scrolling *(from baseline)*

The user reads text content sequentially or by scanning headings. They scroll vertically (and sometimes horizontally) to access content beyond the viewport. They expect content to flow logically top-to-bottom, left-to-right (in LTR languages).

**AT Mechanisms**: Visual reading, Mouse wheel / scroll gesture
**Device context**: All

**UI Requirements**: Text must be readable: sufficient contrast, reasonable font size, adequate line spacing. Content should not require horizontal scrolling at standard viewport widths. Long pages should provide navigation aids (table of contents, back-to-top).

### Form interaction *(from baseline)*

The user fills out forms by clicking into fields, typing, selecting from dropdowns, checking boxes, and submitting. They expect clear labels, helpful placeholder text (that doesn't replace labels), and immediate feedback on errors.

**AT Mechanisms**: Keyboard (typing), Mouse (clicking fields), Visual reading (labels)
**Device context**: All

**UI Requirements**: Every form field must have a visible label. Required fields must be clearly indicated. Error messages must be specific, adjacent to the field, and visible without scrolling. Successful submission must be clearly confirmed.

### Navigation and wayfinding *(from baseline)*

The user navigates between pages/views using menus, breadcrumbs, links, and browser back/forward. They build a mental model of the application's structure. They expect to know where they are, where they can go, and how to get back.

**AT Mechanisms**: Visual scanning, Mouse clicking, Browser controls
**Device context**: All

**UI Requirements**: Consistent navigation across pages. Current location clearly indicated (active nav item, breadcrumbs). Important actions reachable from any page. Browser back button should work predictably.

### Touch / pointer interaction *(from baseline)*

The user taps, swipes, pinch-zooms, and long-presses on a touchscreen to interact with elements. There is no hover state — the user cannot preview interactivity before activating. Touch targets must be large enough to hit accurately. Gestures (swipe, pull-to-refresh) must be discoverable and have single-pointer alternatives.

**AT Mechanisms**: Touchscreen tap, Swipe gestures, Pinch-zoom
**Device context**: Mobile

**UI Requirements**: Touch targets are easy to activate without accidentally activating adjacent controls — small or tightly packed targets force users to retry or hit the wrong control. No reliance on hover for essential information. Custom gestures must have single-pointer alternatives. Drag-and-drop must have tap-based alternatives. No accidental activation from scroll gestures.

## User Variants

### User with assistive hearing device

Uses hearing aids or a cochlear implant that amplifies and processes audio. Speech is usually intelligible in quiet conditions with direct audio streaming (Bluetooth), but degrades in noisy environments, with poor audio quality, multiple overlapping speakers, or unfamiliar accents. Benefits from noise cancellation, clear audio encoding, and adjustable volume. Uses captions as a supplement when conditions are difficult, not as a sole channel. Audio quality matters significantly — compression artifacts, echo, and low bitrate are amplified by the hearing device and degrade intelligibility. High-frequency alert tones may still be missed if they fall above the device's correction range.

**Primary AT**: Hearing aids and cochlear implants, Caption systems (supplementary)
**Device context**: All
**Preferred navigation**: Visual scanning and layout comprehension, Mouse / pointer interaction, Checking audio quality and volume controls

**Expectations**:
- Audio output is clean and well-encoded (no heavy compression, echo, or low bitrate)
- Volume controls are available and go loud enough
- Background noise can be reduced or separated from speech
- Captions available as an optional supplement for difficult listening conditions
- Audio alerts are at perceivable frequencies and volumes, with visual backups
- Voice/video calls offer noise cancellation and audio quality settings
- Mono audio option is available for users with asymmetric hearing loss

### User without assistive hearing device

Has hearing loss but does not use hearing aids or cochlear implants — either by choice, because devices are not suitable for their type of hearing loss, or because they haven't yet been fitted. Relies entirely on the interface's own audio controls and enhancements: system volume, application volume boost, equaliser settings, mono audio, and captions as a primary supplement. More dependent on visual redundancy than the device user because there is no external amplification. May turn volume very high to compensate, potentially affecting others nearby in shared spaces.

**Primary AT**: Enhanced audio settings, Caption systems (supplementary), Mono audio output
**Device context**: All
**Preferred navigation**: Visual scanning and layout comprehension, Mouse / pointer interaction, Looking for captions as supplement

**Expectations**:
- Volume controls are prominent and offer significant boost beyond default
- Captions available and toggleable for all video/audio content
- Visual indicators for all sounds and audio alerts
- Mono audio option available system-wide and in the application
- Audio does not auto-play at unexpected volumes
- Quiet or subtle audio cues always have a visual backup
- Speech in video/audio content is not buried under music or sound effects

### New / infrequent user *(from baseline)*

Encountering the interface for the first time or after a long gap. Relies on visual affordances, labels, and progressive disclosure to learn the interface. Cannot recall shortcuts or hidden features.

**Primary AT**: None
**Device context**: All
**Preferred navigation**: Visual scanning and layout comprehension, Navigation and wayfinding, Form interaction

**Expectations**:
- Interface is learnable without documentation
- Primary actions are visually obvious
- Error messages guide toward resolution
- Navigation structure is understandable

### Expert / frequent user *(from baseline)*

Uses the interface regularly and has built a mental model. Relies on consistency, shortcuts, and efficient workflows. Notices and is disrupted by changes to established patterns.

**Primary AT**: None
**Device context**: All
**Preferred navigation**: Mouse / pointer interaction, Navigation and wayfinding

**Expectations**:
- Keyboard shortcuts for common tasks
- Consistent layout across sessions and pages
- Efficient paths to frequently used features
- Changes to familiar workflows are clearly communicated

## Learning Considerations

- **Discovering audio enhancement settings**: The user needs to find volume controls, equaliser settings, mono audio toggles, and noise cancellation options. If these are buried in system accessibility settings rather than surfaced in the application's audio controls, the user may not discover them. Applications that provide their own audio enhancement options (volume boost, noise reduction) should make them discoverable from the audio/video player or call interface.

- **Caption toggle discovery**: Unlike the deaf persona who always needs captions, the hard-of-hearing user toggles captions based on conditions. They need to find the caption toggle quickly when they start missing words — which may be mid-video or mid-call. If the toggle requires pausing, opening a menu, and navigating to a settings page, the user misses content while searching for it. Caption controls should be immediately accessible during playback.

- **Audio quality calibration**: On first use of a voice/video call feature, the user assesses audio quality and adjusts their hearing device or volume accordingly. If audio quality is inconsistent (varies between calls, changes when someone shares screen, or degrades when bandwidth drops), the user must repeatedly recalibrate. Consistent, high-quality audio reduces this cognitive overhead.

- **Learning which sounds have visual backups**: The user discovers through experience which audio cues have visual equivalents and which don't. If some notifications are reliably paired with visual indicators and others aren't, the user develops anxiety about missing the unreliable ones. Consistent pairing of audio and visual indicators builds confidence; inconsistency creates stress.

- **Volume management in shared spaces**: When the user compensates for hearing loss by turning volume high, they may inadvertently expose private content to others nearby. They learn which environments are safe for loud audio and when to switch to captions-only or text alternatives. The interface's volume and caption controls affect this privacy trade-off.

- **First visit** *(from baseline)*: The user approaches the interface with expectations set by common platform conventions (web, desktop, mobile). Standard UI patterns (hamburger menu, search icon, tabs, modals) are recognized immediately. Non-standard patterns require learning.

- **Mental models** *(from baseline)*: Users build a model of how the interface works based on early interactions. If the model is violated later (e.g., a button that sometimes navigates and sometimes submits), trust erodes and the user becomes cautious and slower.

- **Error recovery** *(from baseline)*: All users occasionally make mistakes. The interface should make errors recoverable (undo, confirmation dialogs, clear error messages) rather than punishing. Irreversible actions without confirmation are universally problematic.

- **Cognitive load** *(from baseline)*: Every interface competes for the user's attention. Cluttered layouts, excessive options, and unclear hierarchy increase cognitive load for everyone, not just users with cognitive disabilities.

## Heuristic Lens Overrides

### Heuristic 1 — Visibility of System Status

Status feedback conveyed through sound — notification chimes, completion tones, error sounds — may be partially heard, heard intermittently, or missed entirely depending on conditions. The user needs visual redundancy for every audio status cue, not because they can never hear it, but because they cannot hear it reliably. Feedback that is audio-only in quiet conditions (where the user hears it) becomes invisible in noisy conditions (where they don't). Visual status indicators must be present and visible regardless of whether the user's current environment allows them to hear the audio cue.

### Heuristic 2 — Match Between System and Real World

Audio content quality must match the user's processing capability. Speech that is clear, well-paced, and free from background noise matches the user's ability to process audio with hearing devices. Audio that is compressed, noisy, fast-paced, or mixed with background music mismatches — the user hears it but cannot process it reliably. For audio/video content, this means: professional audio production quality, clear speech, and separate audio tracks for speech and non-speech content when possible.

### Heuristic 3 — User Control and Freedom

The user must be able to control audio output extensively: adjust volume, toggle captions, switch between stereo and mono, enable noise cancellation, and control playback speed (slower speech is easier to follow). These controls must be accessible during playback, not only in advance. The user should be able to pause and replay audio they missed. For calls, they need control over their own audio input/output settings without disrupting the call.

### Heuristic 4 — Consistency and Standards

Audio quality and visual redundancy should be consistent across the interface. If some videos have captions and others don't, or if some notifications have visual indicators and others are sound-only, the user cannot rely on a consistent strategy for accessing information. Consistent audio encoding quality across the platform prevents the user from having to recalibrate their hearing device or volume for each piece of content.

### Heuristic 5 — Error Prevention

Audio-only error indicators that the user may or may not hear are unreliable error prevention. An error tone the user misses in a noisy environment means the error goes unnoticed until later consequences. Visual error indicators must always accompany audio ones. For forms, inline validation that provides both visual and (optional) audio feedback is ideal — the visual component is the reliable channel, and the audio is a supplement.

### Heuristic 6 — Recognition Rather than Recall

Audio enhancement controls (volume boost, captions, mono audio, noise cancellation) must be discoverable in context. The user should not have to recall that caption settings are in Accessibility > Audio > Captioning > Settings when they need to toggle captions during a video call. Frequently used audio controls should be surfaced in the media player or call interface, not buried in system preferences.

### Heuristic 7 — Flexibility and Efficiency of Use

The interface should support the user's variable hearing ability across conditions. In quiet conditions with a good audio connection, the user may not need captions; in noisy conditions or with poor audio quality, captions become essential. Quick-toggle controls for captions and volume adjustment support this flexibility. The user should not have to navigate away from the current task to adjust audio settings.

### Heuristic 8 — Aesthetic and Minimalist Design

For this persona, audio clutter — background music that competes with speech, ambient sounds that add atmosphere but reduce speech clarity, multiple simultaneous audio streams — is a functional barrier, not an aesthetic choice. Audio design should prioritise speech clarity. Optional audio elements (background music, sound effects) should be separately controllable or disableable. Captions, when enabled, should be styled to be readable without being visually overwhelming.

### Heuristic 9 — Help Users Recognize, Diagnose, and Recover from Errors

When the user misses an audio error cue, they may not know something went wrong until they encounter the downstream consequence. Visual error indicators with clear, specific messages are essential. For calls: if audio quality degrades (the user hears garbled speech), the system should indicate the quality issue visually — the user may not be sure whether they're experiencing hearing difficulty or a network problem. "Your connection quality is poor" helps them diagnose the source.

### Heuristic 10 — Help and Documentation

Documentation should be available in text form, not only as video or audio tutorials. If video tutorials exist, they must have captions and clear audio. Help content explaining audio enhancement features (how to enable noise cancellation, adjust equaliser, toggle mono audio, customise captions) should be easy to find. For hearing device users, documentation about Bluetooth audio streaming compatibility or optimised audio settings is valuable.
